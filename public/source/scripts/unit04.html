Unit IV

Uncial and Half-Uncial

Home › Unit IV

📜 Key Takeaways

  • Uncial was the most widespread book script of late antiquity (c. 350–800 CE), with about 600 surviving codices, and was strongly preferred for Christian texts.
  • Half-uncial is a calligraphic book hand developed from new Roman cursive. About 160 examples survive.
  • Both scripts contributed to subsequent writing traditions: uncial to insular and Carolingian display scripts; half-uncial directly to insular and Carolingian minuscule.

Uncial: Origins and Early Phase

The beginning of uncial script is lost in time, but a fragmentary text known as the De bellis macedonicis has served as a frequent starting point for discussion. On the basis of internal references, the text — which is not otherwise known — is identified by that title. It is one of our earliest literary texts on parchment. Originally dated to the third century CE (when parchment had become more common), arguments about its script morphology and use of the interpunct have urged an earlier date, even as early as the late first century CE (Mallon). Regular use of the interpunct ceases in the second century CE, though later manuscripts in rustic book capitals — such as the Medici Vergil (late fifth century) — employ it consistently, perhaps as a deliberate archaism. The early date of the De bellis macedonicis fragment is still widely accepted.

Parchment fragment with unknown text called De bellis macedonicis. Forerunner of uncials?

Like rustic book capitals, the letters of this fragment are written in a bilinear space and show a contrast between a heavy diagonal moving from upper left to lower right and a light diagonal moving from upper right to lower left. Differences, however, are apparent. The M is reduced from four to three strokes, suggesting a cursive element. The L is reduced from two strokes to one. The serifs have disappeared, surviving perhaps in P and T. More significantly, d, h, and q have completely different forms: d and q are each reduced to two strokes — a bowl and a long diagonal from upper left to lower right — while h lacks the upper portion of the right vertical stroke. These letters more closely resemble variant forms, or allographsAlternative forms of a single letter used within the same script, often varying by position, function, or scribal habit., found in old Roman cursive. The letters a, p, and t also show possible connections to ORC. The remaining letters look more closely related to rustic book capitals. For B, no judgement can be made, as there is no instance of it in this sample. One might say that the scribe attempted to use a calligraphic script written at greater speed than rustic book capitals. The script or one closely related to it probably lies at the origin of the widespread book hand known as uncial, whose earliest records come from the second half of the fourth century.

Uncial writing falls into two phases: an early one extending from approximately 350 to 500 CE, and a later one from about 500 onward. The earlier group shows many connections to North Africa. The major witnesses of the earliest stratum include: the letters of Cyprian, the so-called k-Gospel now in Turin, and the St. Petersburg copy of Augustine's De doctrina christiana, which may have been used in Augustine's own time. The Vercelli Gospels, associated with Eusebius of Vercelli, belong to this early phase but do not display plain North African connections. In the earliest examples: h is formed of two strokes fused into a single curve; m still begins with a vertical downstroke and is not yet rounded; the tail of q is vertical; e resembles a rounded old Roman cursive E with an open eye; the bow of a is pointed; u/v is rounded.

A leaf of the Vercelli Gospels. Vercelli, Biblioeca Capitolare, codex A. Image available at Wikimedia Commons.

Later Uncial

A later form of uncial appears around 500 CE. The rounding of letters, already present in the earlier phase, is accentuated in a, c, d, e, m, o, q, s, and u. The enlargement of the eye in a and the bows in b, p, and q intensifies this effect: the bow of a is round, larger, and less pinched; the bow of d is larger with an upper arm; the bottom arm of F sits on or just above the baseline, and its initial vertical stroke descends below it; the tail of G is now a diagonal (where earlier it was a hook); the baseline stroke of L is more pronounced; M is more rounded; the bows of P and Q fill the middle zone.

While still predominantly a bilinear script, certain features tended over time to be organized over an implicit four-line scaffold. The example below was written around 547 CE in Capua, southern Italy. The letters d, h, and l show some ascenders; f, p, and q have descenders; R may occasionally extend into the lower zone.

Uncial sample from 546 or soon after. Fulda, Hessische Landesbibliothek, Bonifatianus 1 (Codex Fuldensis, Victor Codex), fol. 21r. Image from the Fuldaer Digitale Sammlungen.

Variant Uncial Types

B-R Uncial

A variant of uncial letters from the eastern part of the Roman Empire is called B-R uncial because of the distinctive appearance of these two letters. The upper bow of B rises above the headline. The bow of R fills almost the entire middle zone; its vertical is upright and descends below the baseline; its tail is almost horizontal on the baseline. The De bellis macedonicis fragment already provides a good precedent for this R. The only surviving copies of Vergil in any uncial script use B-R uncial, and both contain signs that they were intended for Greek-speaking readers. B-R uncial appears frequently in legal codices, notably the Codex Florentinus of the sixth century.

An example of B-R uncial. The Codex Florentinus (Florence, Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, s.n.)

b-d Uncial

Another variant, connected with Greek-speaking readers, is named b-d uncial for its notable letters. The script is mainly uncial, but b and d deviate from other uncial models and have forms that probably derive ultimately from new Roman cursive — and may have been adopted from contemporary half-uncial writing, which is itself a calligraphic development of NRC. The principal witness is the Codex Claromontanus, a bilingual Greco-Latin manuscript usually ascribed to southern Italy, though Radiciotti has proposed an eastern, perhaps Egyptian, origin. A related example, the gospel book known as the Codex Bezae, has been located in Gaul, Italy, and Egypt by different scholars.

An example of b-d uncial. A Greek–Latin bilingual edition of the letters of Paul. Paris, B.N.F., grec 107, fol. 364r (Codex Claromontanus). Image from Gallica.

The Spread and Decline of Uncial

From the late fourth century, uncial writing was the most common book hand of the Latin West, and was seemingly preferred for Christian texts — scriptural, theological, and devotional. Merely two extant fragments show rustic capitals used for the Bible. From the early seventh century, some regionally distinguishable varieties developed: Roman uncials (notably the manuscript now in Troyes associated with Gregory the Great), a small group of Spanish uncials, and a considerable group of English uncials, the outstanding example of which is the Codex Amiatinus, itself based on Italian models. By the end of the eighth century, the use of uncials was primarily confined to a display function.

Half-Uncial

Half-uncial writing is a calligraphic version of new Roman cursive. As a book hand, it reduces the frequency of ligatures and decelerates in comparison with its cursive source. About 150 examples survive, compared to about 600 uncial codices.

A "transitional" example provides the starting point: an epitome of Livy's Roman history, usually dated to c. 300 CE. The text occupies the category of book hands, not documentary scripts. In it, the chiaroscuro has decisively changed: once the heaviest stroke, the diagonal is now the finest. The differences from uncial are most visible in b, d, m, and r.

Two varieties of half-uncial can be distinguished. An earlier (or eastern) variety surfaces in the eastern Roman Empire after 300 CE and lasts until about 550; it survives in 49 examples (Licht, 64). The influence of Roman capitals is still conspicuous. A later variety appears after c. 400 and lasts until c. 800. It employs NRC minuscule forms more thoroughly, notably for a, f, g, m, s, and t. The capital form of N is retained in both varieties.

The Eastern Half-Uncial

A script type appearing in Latin sources from the eastern Empire resembles the Livy Epitome but is distinguished by a right inclination. A classic example is the legal text now called the Formula fabiana (CLA VIII, 1042). The text had been dated to the fourth century; a recent reappraisal by Tino Licht proposes a date around 500 CE.

Slanted eastern half-uncial. Berlin, Staatliche Museen P. 11753 + P. 21294 +Vienna, Österreichische Nationalbibliothek, L 90 (Formula Fabiana) .

The eastern half-uncial is mainly a legal script and appears as a display script in the Codex Florentinus (fols. 11v–19v), written mainly in B-R uncial in the sixth century, perhaps soon after 533 CE. The t is bowed, as becomes common after 500; s is minuscule. Uncial forms of B, d, m, and R appear throughout.

The Western Half-Uncial

The later or western half-uncial, found mostly in codices from the western Empire, begins appearing in the fifth century. The so-called papyri barcinonenses (Barcelona papyri, CLA Suppl. 1782) may represent the old western half-uncial. Licht dates them to the early fifth century by analysis of the nomina sacra — the system of abbreviating the names of God — and suggests that Africa may be the point of origin for the whole western tradition. The letter a is uncial; g is a flat-topped minuscule. The scribe makes greater use of ligatures than later scribes, which again suggests derivation from cursive hands.

One of the surviving Barcelona papyri. Image available at Duke Library, P.Duk.inv. 798.

The earliest datable witness of the later western half-uncial is Vatican, B.A.V., Arch. Cap. S. Pietro, D.182, containing theological and polemical works of Hilary of Poitiers. It has been connected to Christian exiles from Africa living at Cagliari, Sardinia, and possibly to Fulgentius of Ruspe (462–527 CE). It is dated by a colophon to 509/510 CE. Folios 13r–27v and 34r–288r are written in half-uncial; folios 288v–311v contain a theological work in uncials. The half-uncial shows minuscule g, straight minuscule s, and a rounded t.

Earliest datable western half-uncial. Vatican, B.A.V., Arch.Cap.S.Pietro, D.182, fol. 13r. Image available at the digital collection of the B.A.V.

The Later History of Half-Uncial

Toward the late sixth century, scribes trained in both uncial and half-uncial increasingly introduced uncial forms into half-uncial texts. The letter N is a telling indicator: in the canonical half-uncial script it has the capital form, with the first shaft sometimes descending below the baseline in earlier examples and both shafts resting on the baseline in later ones. In the late sixth century, a minuscule n entered half-uncial use. At the same time, the upper horizontal strokes of g and t acquired, in one significant half-uncial style based at Castellum Lucullanum (near Naples), an approaching loop on the left side.

Half-uncial left enduring marks on insular majuscule (Irish half-uncial) and on Carolingian minuscule, as well as on the display scripts of early medieval codices.
