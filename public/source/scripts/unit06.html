Unit VI

Merovingian and Precaroline Minuscule

Home › Unit VI

📜 Key Takeaways

  • New Roman cursive served as the basis of numerous regional scripts that emerged in Latin Europe from around 600 CE.
  • Merovingian script ranges from royal charter hands (with dramatically elongated upper zones) to more settled book hands at specific abbeys.
  • The latter half of the eighth century saw an abundance of distinct script types at individual abbeys; none became widely dominant before Caroline minuscule emerged.
  • In Switzerland, Rhaetian and Alemannic minuscules developed under the influence of North Italian cursive; the Alemannic script persisted until around 840 CE.

The Transition from New Roman Cursive

As the documentary and everyday script of the Late Roman Empire, new Roman cursive served as the basis of a number of regional scripts that emerged throughout Latin Europe. Yet the transition between NRC and its successor scripts is not easily observed. Some scholars identify a codex containing homilies by Avitus of Vienne — which preserves a date of c. 520 CE as the earliest possible moment of its creation — as late NRC; others call it Merovingian. The codex survives only as fragments, and has the unusual feature of being written on papyrus rather than parchment. The actual date of its production may be up to a hundred years later than the homilies' delivery.

Sample of New Roman Cursive or Merovingian book hand. Homilies of Avitus of Vienne. Paris, Bibliothèque Nationale de France, Lat. 8913 and Lat. 8914 (520–620 C.E.)

The minuscule of this codex is born from new Roman cursive. The ascenders are elongated and lean slightly to the left. The lower zone for descenders is, by contrast, shallow. Ligatures are less frequent than in NRC, but many survive for o, r and t. There is a suprascript u. A tr-ligature shows a form of t that would become common in other scripts, with the letter t turned vertically on its side. The letter a can behave similarly in ligature. The letter d can be open when in ligature, notably in quod as in later examples. A distinctive ligature ex — with the second diagonal of x formed as a push-stroke from the baseline termination of e — resurfaces in later Merovingian documents. While the debt to NRC is clear, the script has been adapted for book production: the letters are wider and more expanded, even if word separation is not yet consistently observed.

Merovingian Documentary Script

Merovingian documentary writing appears in fifty-four surviving documents. Of them, thirty-eight are royal charters, four royal of these dated before 635 CE. The script is wavy, energetic, tightly compressed, and almost unstable in appearance. The upper zone is markedly elongated, and the top line of the text space receives particular elaboration. A distinctive feature is a minuscule b outfitted with an arm extending to the right, typically forming ligatures with the following letter. While this specific ligature formation did not endure long, the form influenced eighth-century b-shapes.

The Luxeuil Minuscule

The charter script resembles a type associated with the abbey of LuxeuilA Burgundian abbey founded by the Irish missionary Columban c. 590 CE, which became a major center of book production and developed a distinctive minuscule script in the seventh and eighth centuries.. A distinctive type of display capital appears there by 669 CE, and by the first half of the eighth century a distinctive minuscule accompanies it. The Luxeuil minuscule survives in about thirty witnesses.

It is rich in ligatures, particularly those formed from c, e, f, g, o, r, and t. Characteristic letter forms include: a cc-form; b usually closed and often with a right arm for ligature; c possibly in a broken-back form (two strokes joined at the upper left); d in both straight-back and curved-back forms; o with a right arm for ligatures; t in both its standard form with a left loop and, when in ligature, in a rotated form. The principal example is the Lectionary of Luxeuil.

Luxeuil minuscule in the Lectionary of Luxeuil. Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, lat. 9427, fol. 12r. Available via Gallica.

Related to this is the script used in a late seventh-century copy of Gregory of Tours' History of the Franks.

Merovingian book hand, Paris, BNF, lat. 17655, fol. 54r. Image provided by Gallica.

The a and o are more angular. The script recalls charter forms with a sinuous and unstable quality, even adapted as it is to book use. It is replete with ligatures: fi, fe, re, tq, and many others. This manuscript was probably written at the abbey of Corbie, a daughter house of Luxeuil, which explains the graphic connection.

Script Types of the Later Eighth Century

The latter half of the eighth century is a period of experimentation, with the proliferation of many distinct script types, none of which became widespread. A script type, in this context, is characterized by consistent distinctive features executed by more than one hand over a period of time. Such features that apply as much to the ornamental and display elements as to the main text. The following table highlights the distinctive features of each type.

The Laon a-z Type

A close relative of the Luxeuil tradition is the so-called Laon a-z type, extant in only eight manuscripts dated to the third quarter of the eighth century. Its main features are: an a formed from two pinched c's; and a z with a very high horn at the upper right. The letter i stands frequently in ligature. The letters d and q often have an open bow after e and o. The soft ti (pronounced with a sibilant) is formed by the ligature of t with a long descending i; the hard (unassibilated) ti is formed with a vertically rotated t and a normal i.

Chelles: b-type Minuscule and N-uncial

At the abbey of Chelles, two distinctive house types appeared in the second half of the eighth century: the b-type minuscule and the N-uncial. Both can be seen together on a page of the Gelasian Sacramentary (Vatican, BAV, Reg. lat. 316, fol. 2v). In the b-type minuscule: a has several variants — a form with a horn at the upper left, cc-, and oc-versions, and a suprascript form in ligature; b has a cross-stroke on the stem; r forms a ligature at the baseline. The accompanying N-uncial is characterized by: D with an uncial form resembling an apple with a stem; G with a sinuous tail; N with its second upright tipped backward, sometimes with a small foot at the lower right; S with a small top curve; X with a high junction point and a wavy left stroke.

Chelles N-uncial with b-type minuscule above. Gelasian Sacramentary, BAV, Reg. lat. 316, fol. 2v: digi.vatlib.it.

Corbie Script Types

Several scribal developments have been connected to the abbey of Corbie during the eighth century. In the e-N minuscule, e is tall and shows an open upper loop in ligature; N is often written in uncial rather than minuscule form. The letter a is open; y is occasionally dotted, with both arms curving to the right.

Sample of e-N minuscule. Cassiodorus, Commentary on the Psalms. Paris, BNF, lat. 12239, fol. 1v-2r. Available at Gallica.

A later type called the ab-type appears in about forty codices, some linked to the abbey of Soissons, some bearing the names of female scribes in the quire signatures. According to Bischoff, it draws on Merovingian chancery forms. The a is formed of i + c; b has a round but open bowl with a right stroke in the shaft — a vestige of the Merovingian b. The surviving witnesses date to 790–830, when Caroline minuscule was already becoming established; the script looks like a throwback to Merovingian forms.

Rhaetian and Alemannic Minuscule

Independent of developments in Gaul, another script type emerged in what is now Switzerland, at the confluence of North Italian and Gallic currents around the bishopric of Chur. The Rhaetian script shows a slight right inclination. Its characteristic letter forms include: a cc-a with occasional Caroline uncial a; upright g; o sometimes resembling a reversed uncial d; ri-ligature; t with a closed bow on the left, executed in three movements with the top element in two strokes; and a us-ligature from the Merovingian period.

Sample of Rhaetian script. Sankt Gallen, Stiftsbibliothek, 348, p. 368. Image available at e-codices.unifr.ch.

A successor script, Alemannic minuscule, was written at the abbeys of Saint Gall and the Reichenau in the latter part of the eighth century until being supplanted by Caroline minuscule around 840 CE. About 100 examples survive. Its most notable features are an nt-abbreviation that appears not only at word-end but also within words; abbreviations of -mus and -nus at word-end; and a word-final -tur abbreviation written as an apostrophe over t.

An early Alemannic hand is represented by the scribe Winithar of Saint Gall (c. 740–c. 790), whose hand appears in at least seven manuscripts.

Winithar's address to his fellow monks, giving his own name. Saint Gall, Stiftsbibliothek, 70, p. 250. Image from e-codices.

Winithar's writing leans to the right and gives an impression of instability. The strokes are heavy and the letter modulus large; at its most calligraphic, the letters stand upright without leaning. A later example (Saint Gall, Stiftsbibliothek, 914, p. 50) shows a tendency toward more upright letters: a less scrawling, finer script that retains the large modulus and characteristic ligatures and abbreviations, but with calligraphic ideals of uniformity and display. Word separation is markedly better and approaches a division into syllables.

Alemannic minuscule from the first third of the ninth century. Saint Gall, Stiftsbibliothek, 914, p. 50. Image from e-codices.unifr.ch.
