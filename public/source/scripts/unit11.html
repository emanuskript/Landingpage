Unit XI

Gothic Cursive Hands

Home › Unit XI

📜 Key Takeaways

  • Gothic cursive book hands are distinguished from textualis by specific morphological features, above all in the construction of a, f, s, and the treatment of ascenders.
  • The Lieftinck-Derolez classification distinguishes six types (textualis, semitextualis, cursiva antiquior, cursiva recentior, hybrida, and bastarda) across three grades of execution (formata, libraria, currens).
  • Regional stylizations — cancelleresca (Italy), bastarda (France and Flanders), and Secretary (England) — developed from cursiva recentior.

What is "Cursive" in Late-Medieval Palaeography?

Before discussing the cursive scripts found in late-medieval books alongside textualis, it is important to clarify the concept of cursivity. In palaeography, the term "cursive" traditionally refers to the scripts of everyday writing and of documents: scripts developed to record a text as economically as possible, primarily in terms of time, often at a cost to legibility. Such scripts tend to appear aesthetically rough and to be rich in ligatures and abbreviations.

Although less well represented in scholarly literature, the cursive level of writing has always coexisted alongside calligraphic book hands, and it is precisely within the everyday dimension that the determining elements of change in a graphic system tend to originate. For the earlier phases, our understanding is constrained by the limited survival of such material; innovations become visible to us principally once they have been formalized and stabilized in book hands.

The relationship between cursive and book hand can be illustrated with a metaphor. Cursive scripts may be compared to the roots of a tree: vitally important yet largely invisible, because everyday records were generally ephemeral — drafts on wax tablets, or documents that became invalid after a certain date. The visible parts of the tree — trunk and leaves — correspond to book hands, preserved because they were used in books intended to last. The trunk represents cursive scripts adapted for book use; the leaves represent the most calligraphic outcomes, refined to the point of eliminating visible cursive features.

The term "cursive" in palaeography also appears in two further senses:

  • It can refer to the level of execution, denoting scripts written quickly and fluently — in which case it defines what is not calligraphic.
  • It can refer to the level of morphology, describing scripts of cursive origin that, through adaptation for books, appear more restrained and legible than ordinary documentary forms.

The Gothic cursive book hands discussed in this unit are cursive in the second sense: they consist of letterforms originating in documentary scripts, later adapted for book use through calligraphic reworking. They derive from a cursive variant of Caroline minuscule developed by notaries as early as the twelfth century to accelerate document production.

The Transformation into Book Scripts

To improve clarity and legibility, scribes progressively simplified cursive letterforms, reshaping them to resemble textualis more closely. The changes introduced included:

  • Reduction and simplification of the numerous complex ligatures typical of cursive scripts;
  • Breaking the uninterrupted cursive ductus into shorter strokes;
  • Shortening ascenders and introducing letter fusions to make writing more compact;
  • Adopting letterforms typical of textualis alongside cursive variants.

Because of this transformation, many scholars describe these scripts as "Gothic cursives." The phenomenon is complex and heterogeneous: it is not always easy to determine whether influence flows from textualis to cursive or from cursive to textualis, and the scripts vary considerably by region.

The Lieftinck-Derolez Classification

Palaeographers have long disagreed about how to interpret and classify Gothic cursive book hands. Some employ the generic label "bastard scripts" or adopt definitions valid only within specific national contexts, arguing that a universally accepted formal description is either unattainable or unnecessary. Others have sought frameworks based on objective criteria with the aim of standardizing terminology.

The most complete formal classification is the Lieftinck system, as supplemented by Derolez. It operates on two levels:

  • Level 1 (morphology). Depending on the presence or absence of specific morphological features, three types are distinguished: textualis, cursiva, hybrida. This level can be considered objective, as it rests on quantitative principles.
  • Level 2 (execution). Three grades of execution are distinguished: formata (most calligraphic), libraria (everyday book hand), and currens (rapid). This level involves subjective judgment, as no widely accepted method for measuring execution objectively has been established.

Derolez subsequently proposed additions to Lieftinck's morphological classification, resulting in a model comprising six graphic types: textualis, semitextualis, cursiva antiquior, cursiva recentior, hybrida, and bastarda.

Semitextualis

In the Lieftinck-Derolez classification, semitextualis is applied to book scripts of the thirteenth through fifteenth centuries closely related to textualis but distinguished above all by the consistent use of the single-compartment a. Originally associated with scripts used by Francesco Petrarch, semitextualis is a typically Italian phenomenon, known in Italian palaeography as semigotica, and was used for both Latin and vernacular texts. Outside Italy, it is attested only sporadically in Spain, Portugal, and southern France. While Lieftinck-Derolez classifies it as a "descendant" of textualis, the Italian palaeographer Paola Supino Martini has argued that it derives more from documentary cursive hands, a view supported by the manner in which ligatures are formed.

Cursiva: Antiquior and Recentior

Cursiva is morphologically distinguished from textualis chiefly by the presence of looped ascenders on b, h, k, and l, and by descenders on f and s that extend below the baseline. Two subcategories are distinguished:

Cursiva Antiquior

Cursiva antiquior retains the two-compartment a, a feature that keeps it closer to textualis than other cursive types. It originated in England in the thirteenth century, developed from the local documentary script of the royal chancery. During the fourteenth century it spread to northern France, the German-speaking countries, and Scandinavia, but it never reached southern Europe or the Low Countries. The only fully canonized script derived from cursiva antiquior is Anglicana, which appeared in England in the thirteenth century and remained in use until the sixteenth. Anglicana was made famous by the studies of the English palaeographer Malcolm Parkes.

Anglicana example: Cambridge, Gonville and Caius College, MS 59/153. Cambridge Digital Library

Cursiva Recentior

Cursiva recentior is the book type most widely used in Europe between the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. It is distinguished from the antiquior group specifically by the single-compartment a. It is the most widespread European Gothic cursive, though specific regional stylizations can be identified. These stylizations generally retain the formal and artificial character of local chancery scripts from which they derive, and are typical of luxury vernacular manuscripts, though they were also used for Latin texts. Italy, France, and England developed more distinct and recognizable stylizations; Germany used cursiva widely but without stable standardized forms; examples from the Iberian Peninsula are comparatively rare.

Non-calligraphic book cursive (16th century): IIIF manifest (Saint-Omer)

Regional Stylizations of Cursiva Recentior

Cancelleresca (Italy)

As a script for everyday use, cancelleresca is attested as early as the twelfth century in notarial contexts. As a book hand, it was in use from the late thirteenth century to the end of the fourteenth, principally for high-quality literary manuscripts in the vernacular. It is usually associated with the manuscript tradition of Dante's Commedia. Distinctive features include:

  • Lack of inclination (upright letterforms);
  • Thick diagonal strokes;
  • Triangular ("flag-shaped") loops, closed or open, formed by the ascenders of b, h, and l;
  • Looped d, formed by both counterclockwise and clockwise movements;
  • h and m with the final stroke curving below the baseline;
  • Two forms of r: textualis r and 2-shaped r;
  • Tironian et in the shape of a 7;
  • Adjacent ascenders typically connected and written with a simplified ductus;
  • z in the form of ç (hence the spelling Firençe for Firenze).
Dante, Commedia in cancelleresca: Vatican, BAV, Urb. lat. 378, fol. 61r. digi.vatlib.it

Bastarda (France, Flanders, England)

The term "bastarda" in the Lieftinck-Derolez system does not refer generically to all late medieval Gothic cursive book hands, as it does in much palaeographic usage. Here it designates a specific book-hand style developed in the fifteenth century in certain regions of France, characteristic of luxury manuscripts produced at the court of the Dukes of Burgundy — hence also known as "lettre bourguignonne." In addition to Burgundy, Franche-Comté, and Flanders, it was widespread in France generally and was later exported to England. It derives from two main sources: the French book hand of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, and the script of the royal chancery.

Distinctive features include bold and generally slanted f and s, creating marked contrast with the more uniform letterforms; and a pronounced angularity produced by sharp projecting points at the top strokes of certain letters (e.g., e, o, t).

Bastarda, Heures à l'usage d'Amiens: Abbeville, Bibliothèque municipale, MS 16, fol. 6r. IRHT IIIF

Secretary (England)

Secretary script developed in England between the second half of the fourteenth century and the first half of the fifteenth, emerging from centuries of political and cultural contact with France. Influenced by French documentary and book hands, English scribes adapted and simplified the ductus of Anglicana. Malcolm Parkes referred to particularly textualized variants as "Bastard Secretary." The script can appear in a wide range of forms, from very thick strokes to very thin ones. It typically retains certain letterforms inherited from Anglicana, including a 6-shaped final s and an 8-shaped g.

Secretary, medical treatises in English and Latin: London, Wellcome Library, MS 411. Wellcome Digital Library
