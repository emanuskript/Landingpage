Unit IX

Caroline and Pregothic Minuscule

Home › Unit IX

📜 Key Takeaways

  • Caroline minuscule emerged fitfully in the second half of the eighth century and became the dominant book script of Western Europe between 800 and 1200 CE.
  • Its success rested on clarity, a well-organized quadrilinear scaffold, reduced ligatures, and a distinctive uncial a that avoided confusion with similar open forms.
  • Pregothic Caroline minuscule, visible from around 1100, modified the Caroline canon with forked ascenders, hairline additions, and changed feet — signaling the transition to Gothic textualis.

Origins: The Earliest Evidence

During the eighth century, several distinct script types emerged at specific religious houses, and many codices were written in multiple or mixed scripts. The earliest glimpses of Caroline minuscule surface in the interstices of these varied environments — in the margins, corrections, and final pages where other types and contemporary alternatives were set aside. The appearance of this minuscule, fitful as it is, shows well-developed forms, as if the letterforms had originated in untraced ephemera: parchment scraps, wax tablets, and everyday writing practice (see Petrucci, "Book, Handwriting, and School").

The earliest evidence shows variable letterforms. Uncial A, d, G, S, and capital N coexist alongside minuscule a, d, g, n, and s. In the earliest stage, a multiplicity of letterforms was available before the selection settled on uncial a, straight-back minuscule d, minuscule g, and minuscule n.

The Leutchar Type and Berlin MS Theol. Lat. fol. 354

The manuscript Berlin, Staatsbibliothek, MS Theol. Lat. fol. 354 was completed before the death of Leutchar, abbot of Corbie, in 769 CE (Licht, "Die älteste karolingische Minuskel"). The half-uncial on its first page takes the name "Leutchar type" from the abbot. If Caroline minuscule is the product of a minuscule tradition disciplined by half-uncial ideals, the Leutchar type gives an idea of what that disciplining might have involved. It is far more compact than other half-uncial forms, and it is difficult to identify its organizing scaffold as either two-line or four-line. The clubbing on the ascenders marks out an upper zone, but it is compressed.

Half-uncial in Berlin, Staatsbibliothek, MS Theol. Lat. fol. 354, fol. 1r. Available at Staatsbibliothek Digitalisierte Sammlungen.

The script on the following page is plainly different.

Early Caroline minuscule in Berlin, Staatsbibliothek, MS Theol. Lat. fol. 354, fol. 1v. Available at Staatsbibliothek Digitalisierte Sammlungen.

Letter elements are now clearly organized across three zones; the upper zone opens conspicuously. The ascenders have clubbing. Although the ruling is identical to the preceding page, the wider distribution of script zones makes the script lighter. There is a regular minim height; e rises above it when in ligature. The strokes are narrower, with less immediately evident contrast. Among the letterforms: g and n are minuscule (both derived from new Roman cursive); the uncial a has the form of a diminutive uncial, probably to avoid the confusion that oc-, cc-, or open forms could produce (Licht). It is possible that this form had already entered the everyday script, perhaps for the same reason.

The Maurdramnus Bible

An early minuscule form, considered by some scholars a primitive Caroline minuscule, appears in a monumental form in the Maurdramnus Bible, a multi-volume Old Testament of which five volumes are preserved at Amiens. The colophon in Amiens 11 identifies the patron as Abbot Maurdramnus, who headed Corbie until 781. The script was written slowly, probably with a pen held nearly perpendicular to the page. As a large lectern Bible, it is designed for communal reading: letters are large, round, distinct, and regular. About sixty manuscripts survive of this type; some also show, in correctors' or marginal interventions, the Leutchar type and the Corbie ab-type.

Maurdramnus Bible. Amiens, Bibliothèque municipale, 11, fol. 66r. Image available at Gallica.

Caroline Minuscule at the Court of Charlemagne

Caroline minuscule appears in close proximity to the Carolingian court in three luxury biblical manuscripts:

  • The Godescalc Evangeliary (781–783)
  • The Ada Gospels
  • The Dagulf Psalter

The earliest datable witness is the dedicatory page at the end of the Godescalc Evangeliary, executed between 781 and 783. This luxury Gospel book is written in gold uncials on purple-dyed parchment. The minuscule n is regular despite one capital in line 4. The letter A shifts inconsistently between uncial and cc-form. Y is dotted. The ligatures rt and st persist throughout Caroline minuscule, but apart from these the letters are set and non-cursive.

First datable Carolingian minuscule. Dedicatory page of the Godescalc Evangeliary: Paris, BNF, n.a. lat. 1203, fol. 126v. Image available at Gallica.

The origins of this script remain debated. Whether it is a kind of NRC disciplined by half-uncial forms, whether it originated at a single abbey, or whether it developed simultaneously in different places — these questions cannot be answered with certainty. Nor is it fully clear what relationship, if any, this script bore to the educational reforms promoted in Charlemagne's Admonitio generalis, a capitulary issued in 789 calling for higher standards in copying books, above all liturgical books.

The Achievement of Caroline Minuscule

Between 800 and 1200, Caroline minuscule spread across Europe. Bischoff's catalogue of ninth-century manuscripts contains about 9,000 entries, the overwhelming majority representing Caroline minuscule. In the early years, the diffusion may have been aided by the export of high-quality Bibles from Tours. By around 830, during the reign of Louis the Pious, variation in the canon of letterforms had largely stabilized. As an example, one might turn to the Alcuin Bible illuminated at Tours in the 830s. A number of scribes copied this codex. The first shows “archaic” features in their script: minuscule a, g of half-uncial influence shaped like a flat-topped 3, capital N. This hand appears for display purposes through the end of fol. 8r, the first page of Genesis. On the first verso, the scribe uses the minuscule forms of these three letters.

The beginning of a Genesis in a Tours Bible, a display page and a standard page. Bamberg, Staatsbibliothek, Msc.Bibl.1, fol. 8r-8v. Image availbel through the Münchener Digitalisierungszentrum.

According to Malcolm Parkes, the script's success lay in the clarity of its cues to legibility — above all the distinctive elements centered on the middle zone. Cherubini and Pratesi further emphasize the unprecedented regularization of the quadrilinear organizing field around the center. The principal features may be summarized as follows:

  • Organization of elements. A balance of straight and round strokes proportionately organized over three zones. Broad curves and bows (b, d, g, h, p, q); evenly spaced verticals making the minim the building unit of i, m, n, r, u. Letters b, d, l ascend to the top zone; f and s may occupy the lower zone; p, g, q descend; e and t are confined to the middle zone.
  • Clarity. Moderate contrast; narrow nib and strokes; aerated layout (more space between lines); uncompressed (more space between letters) with few ligatures or connections. Ligatures have been reduced and standardized; where they survive, they typically derive from pre-Caroline forms. Common ligatures occur outside the minim height: ct, rt, st.
  • Line definition. Upright verticals with symmetrical clubbing drawn in two movements (b, d, h, l — derived from half-uncial, stylizing the rapid up-and-down movement of NRC). Small approach strokes create "shoulders" on f, p, s, and the minim; some upward finishing strokes at the base.
  • Morphological disambiguation. The various a forms (oc, cc, closed ci, open ic) yield to the uncial a, which avoids confusion with cc, ci, or u. Half-uncial r, which could resemble n, was abandoned in favor of minuscule r.

Certain letters continued to appear in two forms, especially in earlier periods: straight-back and uncial d (before the straight-back form became conventional); i brevis and i-longa (both in use, later i brevis normal); n and N (before n became conventional); and long s with descender alongside uncial s (the latter uncommon).

Petrarch and the Retrospective Appreciation of Caroline

Much later, in a letter of 1366, the Italian poet Francesco Petrarca wrote to Giovanni Boccaccio lamenting the writing styles current in their day. A manuscript sent by Boccaccio to Petrarch — today Paris, BNF, lat. 1989, a copy of Augustine's Commentary on the Psalms — survives with Petrarch's ownership note praising the beauty of the book, its ancient lettering, and the simple elegance of its decoration. The manuscript dates to the last half of the eleventh century.

The eleventh-century Caroline minuscule praised by Petrarch. Paris, BNF, lat. 1989, fol. 2r. Image available at Gallica.

Pregothic Caroline Minuscule

A Vergil manuscript copied around 1140 (Vatican, BAV, Vat. lat. 1580) illustrates Caroline minuscule at the pregothic stage. The morphology of the Caroline canon is still recognizable, yet several elements signal the modification underway: ascenders have become noticeably shorter relative to minim height, especially l and sometimes b; ascenders and minims receive an upward finishing stroke from the baseline; the top stroke of uncial d is long and reaches down; diagonal hairlines are visible in e and g; the ascenders b, straight-backed d, and l are forked at the top; the top of uncial d is almost flattened; the arch of h sometimes turns back to the left at the baseline; x is executed in three strokes.

The beginning of Vergil's Aeneid. Vatican, BAV, Vat. lat. 1580, fol. 40v. Image available at the Vatican Digital Library.
