Unit I

The Origins of the Alphabet, Inscriptions, and Square Capitals

Home › Unit I

📜 Key Takeaways

  • The Latin alphabet derived from the Western Greek alphabet, transmitted through Etruscan intermediaries in the eighth century BCE.
  • Square capitals — based on monumental inscriptions such as the Column of Trajan — a sustained book hand in a limited number of instances.
  • Throughout the Middle Ages, it was used as a high-grade display script rather than a standard book hand.
  • Renaissance scholars recovered square capitals from Roman monuments and theorized their proportions, laying the groundwork for early typography.

The History of the Roman Alphabet

The origin of the Latin alphabet can be traced to the establishment of permanent Greek settlements in southern Italy during the eighth century BCE. The Etruscans of southern Etruria adopted an alphabet around the last quarter of that century under the influence of the Western Greek alphabet. Other peoples of the Italian peninsula, including the Romans, subsequently adopted the alphabet through Etruscan mediation. During the archaic Latin period, beginning in the seventh century BCE, Latin inscriptions were initially written from right to left, following Etruscan practice, or in alternating directions — a method known as boustrophedonFrom the Greek for "ox-turning": a writing pattern in which alternate lines run in opposite directions, as an ox turns at the end of a furrow.. Left-to-right writing gradually became standard by the middle of the Republican period, roughly the third century BCE.

Letter shapes and names underwent significant changes in the exchanges between civilizations. Greeks adopted their alphabet from the Phoenicians and named letters for their initial sounds (e.g., beta for "b"). The Etruscans preserved the order of letters but apparently did not preserve the Greek names. The Romans, in turn, developed a naming system that forms the basis of modern European letter names (a, be, ce, de, and so on). The Greek letter gamma became "C" in the Latin alphabet, where it initially represented both the /k/ and /g/ sounds. In the late third century BCE, a scholar — perhaps Spurius Carvilius Ruga or Appius Claudius — distinguished the /g/ sound by modifying C into a new letter G.

The alphabet went from A to X and lacked distinctive characters for I and J and for U, V and W. The letters Y and Z were added to the Latin alphabet in the first century BCE to accommodate Greek names and loanwords. As recorded by the historians Tacitus and Suetonius, Emperor Claudius (r. 41–54 CE) attempted to introduce three further letters to represent specific sounds, but these were not adopted by subsequent generations.

Ancient and medieval legends offer many accounts for the origin of the Latin alphabet. In one Christian account, Adam foresees the devastating catastrophe, and a later descendant constructs two pillars, inscribing on them the first letters to preserve knowledge (Treffort, 2013).

Roman Inscriptions

Before papyrus or parchment became widely available, the dominant writing media in the Roman world were tablets and inscriptions carved in stone or cast in metal. Wax tablets were used for documents, letters, and school exercises; bronze tablets served primarily legislative purposes. Stone inscriptions survive in several categories: gravestones, altars, honorific monuments, and walls bearing painted or incised text.

Among the oldest surviving Latin inscriptions is the so-called Duenos Vase (CIL 01, 00004), which may date as early as the seventh century BCE. The range of surviving inscriptions is enormous. Personal epitaphs include those for Allia Potestas (CIL 06, 37965), soldiers Quintus Aemilius Secundus (CIL 03, 06687) and Tiberius Julius Abdes Pantera (CIL 08, 07514), and the citizen known as Turia (CIL 06, 37053), all dating roughly to the first century BCE–first century CE. Famous family tombs include those of the Scipios.

Monuments for public display include a calendar inscription of twelve columns (CIL 06, 02305, first century CE), a Roman boundary marker from modern Syria (CIL 03, 00183), a victory altar from Augsburg (AE 1993, 01231, third century CE), and the Column of Goths in modern Istanbul (CIL 03, 00733, fourth century CE). Structures honoring Roman rulers include the Temple of Augustus and Rome (CIL 03, p. 0774), the Arch of Titus (CIL 06, 00945), Trajan's Column (CIL 06, 00960), and the Column of Marcian (CIL 03, 00738).

Marble tablets preserving Roman historical records include the Fasti Triumphales (CIL 01, p. 341), recording military victories; the Fasti Capitolini, covering Republican officials through the time of Augustus; the Fasti Ostienses (CIL 14, 00244), spanning the mid-first century BCE to the late second century CE; and the Fasti Potentini, covering the late first to early second century CE.

Bronze tablets record important legal acts. The most significant is the Laws of the Twelve Tables, the fifth-century BCE foundation of Roman law. Other examples include a decree prohibiting the festivals of Bacchus (second century BCE; CIL 10, 00104), the Tabula Contrebiensis concerning water rights (first century BCE), the Heraclean Tablets on municipal regulations, and the Lex Ursonensis and Lex Irnitana granting citizenship in Iberia. Researchers seeking further inscriptions may consult the Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum (CIL), L'Année épigraphique (AE), and the Epigraphik-Datenbank Clauss/Slaby (EDCS).

Square Book Capitals

One letter canons used on stone inscriptions is the monumental capital. Monumental capitals were adapted as a book script in late antiquity. As a book script they are called square capitals, but this designation acknowledges their origin. The name square capital derives from the Latin litterae quadratae, a term already attested in the first century CE. The writer Petronius probably refers to the same letter form with the expression litterae lapidariae, "letters on stone." The monumental capitals inscribed on stone in the ancient Roman world — most notably those on the Column of Trajan in Rome — served as the model for the book form.

The Column of Trajan, Rome, c. 113 CE. Public domain via Wikimedia Commons.

The inscription.

The inscription on Trajan's Column celebrates the physical achievement of quarrying the stone needed to clear the ground for the forum complex. How exactly such monumental inscriptions were made is a matter of scholarly debate. The letters were probably first outlined with a brush and then incised with a chisel struck by a hammer. Several unfinished inscriptions preserve traces of preliminary brushwork (Catich; Mallon).

It is perhaps misleading to begin an overview of book scripts with square capitals. Square capitals, though based on a widely practiced inscribed script, were used for books only in a limited way. Only three surviving manuscripts hint at an entire book written in square capitals; all three are accidental survivals. One is a scrap of parchment from Egypt (CLA 10.1569); the other two survive as more extensive fragments, one of them reused as bookbinding material (CLA 1.13 + 8.**13; CLA 7.977). All three contain works of the Roman poet Vergil and date between the late fourth and late fifth centuries. Square capitals may have been employed at this time to honor a writer who had attained the status of a classic. Nevertheless, square capitals are treated first here because the script form is the most immediately recognizable to a modern reader. Moreover, the letter forms do appear as though they can be inscribed within a square, a rectangle, or — in the case of the round letters C, D, G, and O — a circle. When the monumental capitals first became book capitals, the letters were not uniformly fitted into a strict proportional grid; being adjusted to a smaller scale, the written forms tend to be broader than the inscribed ones.

The Saint Gall Vergil. Saint Gall, Stiftsbibliothek, 1394, p. 15. Available via e-Codices.

The strokes of the letters regularly move from top to bottom and left to right. This is the script's ductusThe sequence and direction of pen strokes used to construct a letter. Ductus is a fundamental concept in palaeography because it helps identify how a script was written and can distinguish otherwise similar forms., and it becomes the governing rule of most calligraphic book hands throughout the Middle Ages. In square capitals, vertical strokes are heavy; horizontal strokes are fine. The contrast of strokes reflects the angle at which the broad pen was held.

Certain tall letters — I, F, and L — could be written taller than their companions, helping distinguish F from E and L from I. Curves were broken into constituent strokes to maintain the directional rule: C was written in two strokes, G in three. The two strokes of O reveal the full width of the pen nib. The contrast of strokes was an important part of the script aesthetic. The relatively fine vertical strokes of M and N, seemingly at odds with the overall style, may have been made by holding the pen at a steeper angle; these fine strokes introduce contrast, or chiaroscuro, into letters that would not otherwise display it conspicuously.

Square Capitals as a Display Script

As far as the surviving evidence indicates, square capitals did not serve as a conventional book script in the Middle Ages. Throughout the medieval period they remained primarily a display scriptA script used for titles, headings, colophons, or other text elements intended to stand out visually from the main body of a manuscript. Display scripts are typically more formal, larger, or more elaborate than the surrounding text., deployed for titles, headings, or other conspicuous text elements.

Wolfenbüttel, Herzog August Bibliothek, Cod. Guelf. 36.23 Aug. 2°, fol. 36r (image 133). Sixth-century uncial codex on measuring fields, showing square capitals as a display script. Available via HAB digital library.

Square capitals held the highest rank among display scripts in the great pandect Bibles produced at Tours in the ninth century, most notably in the Bibles commissioned for the Emperor Charles the Bald.

Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, lat. 1 (First Bible of Charles the Bald), fol. 4v. Tours, c. 845. Square capitals appear at the top of the page. Via Gallica.

At this time square capitals were probably transmitted through model alphabets. The scholar Lupus of Ferrières (d. 862) obtained such a model from his mentor Einhard around 836. The examplar survives now in Bern. Note how K is formed like a roofless R.

The Renaissance Recovery of Square Capitals

Stroke contrast and rectilinear proportions became central concerns when Renaissance scholars rediscovered this script through direct study of Roman monuments. The antiquary Felice Feliciano (1433–1479) undertook in the mid-fifteenth century what is often regarded as the earliest palaeographical study of Roman monumental capitals.

The letter O in Feliciano's study of Roman inscriptions. Vatican, Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana, Vat. lat. 6852, fol. 7v.

Renaissance scholars drew particular attention to the terminal strokes of letters — the serifsShort finishing strokes at the ends of the main strokes of a letter. In Roman square capitals, serifs probably originated in the brushwork used to sketch letters before they were incised in stone.. These serifs were already present in square book capitals of antiquity. The approach strokes, finish strokes, and supplementary serifs may have originated in the brushwork used to draft characters before incision (Catich). During the mid-fifteenth century, scholars combined the morphology of Roman monumental capitals with a theory of ideal architectural proportions. Luca Pacioli — an acquaintance and collaborator of Leonardo da Vinci in Milan — prepared a study of Roman square capitals first as a manuscript, surviving today only in print. Pacioli envisioned letter forms on a grid and paid close attention to the use of circles and radial constructions to shape the adnate serifsSerifs that join the main stroke along a smoothly developed curve rather than at an abrupt angle. Characteristic of Roman square capitals and later revived in Renaissance and early modern typography..

An illustration from Pacioli's study of Roman capitals, showing letters constructed on a geometric grid. The original manuscript does not survive; the printed edition (De divina proportione, Venice, 1509) contains the letter plates.

These studies, alongside others, formed the basis for a renewal of script styles in manuscripts and on monuments, and for innovation in print typography after c. 1460. In particular, the development of movable type in metal allowed a close formal replication and wide diffusion of engraved serifs.
