Humanist Scripts

Home › Unit XII

📜 Key Takeaways

  • Humanist scripts were a conscious rejection of the Gothic graphic tradition, grounded in the recovery of late Carolingian manuscripts that humanists mistook for genuinely ancient writing.
  • Poggio Bracciolini (1380–1459) created the first manuscripts written entirely in littera antiqua (humanistic minuscule); Niccolò Niccoli (1365–1437) invented humanistic cursive.
  • In the second half of the fifteenth century, humanistic cursive evolved into Italic, which became the dominant European cursive of the sixteenth century and the ancestor of modern italic and cursive hands.

Humanism and the Reform of Script

The term humanistic scripts refers to the scripts associated with the historical and cultural movement of Humanism, which developed in Italy from the mid-fourteenth century and came to an end toward the close of the fifteenth. On a cultural level, the movement was characterized by a systematic return to classical antiquity: the literary works of Greek and Roman authors, largely excluded from early medieval education, were recovered as literary and linguistic models, as ethical examples, and as guides for living. After the fall of the Byzantine Empire to the Ottoman Turks in 1453, many Greek intellectuals sought refuge in Europe and played a decisive role in the rediscovery of Greek culture.

Humanists traveled throughout Italy and Europe in search of ancient manuscripts. The manuscripts they found often dated back no more than two or three centuries, but they appeared far more elegant, clear, and legible than the contemporary Gothic textualis, which was visually demanding because of its artificial features. As a result, humanists gradually moved away from the Gothic graphic tradition and brought late Carolingian script back into use, renaming their version littera antiqua in opposition to textualis — which they called littera moderna. The spread of humanistic scripts was therefore not a natural development within the Gothic writing system, but the result of a conscious cultural choice.

Among the forerunners of this graphic revolution were intellectual figures prominent in the administration and politics of Italian courts: Albertino Mussato and Lovato Lovati in Padua; Landolfo Colonna in Avignon; and Francesco Petrarca, Giovanni Boccaccio, and Coluccio Salutati in Florence. From a graphic point of view, these individuals were trained in the Gothic tradition, but their scripts show new elements reflecting a broader cultural change — to the extent that some of their written examples have been described as semi-Gothic, or, in the specific case of Coluccio Salutati, as pre-antique.

Poggio Bracciolini and the Littera Antiqua

The principal executor of the humanistic graphic reform was Poggio Bracciolini (1380–1459). He was responsible for the first manuscripts written entirely in antiqua, also known as humanistic minuscule. This script was used mainly to produce elegant copies of Latin classical authors, characterized by high-quality parchment and a refined decorative apparatus inspired by the Carolingian tradition.

Coming from a family of the mercantile and notarial class, Poggio's book hand was not originally humanistic; it underwent continuous reworking over the years, aided by his contact with Coluccio Salutati and by the experience he gained through service in the papal chancery and his many journeys across Europe.

His early training script (Fig. 1), used to copy a Ciceronian oration, can be classified as a hybrid. It is characterized by f and s descending below the baseline, shafts without loops, and a strongly cursive ductus. The use of a Roman capital in the rubric is, however, a typically humanistic feature.

Fig. 1 — Poggio, early hand. Vatican, BAV, Vat. lat. 11458. Image available from the Digital Vatican Library.

A manuscript of Valerius Maximus (Fig. 2) represents an initial, fully developed attempt at the littera antiqua, though the textualis matrix remains strong: the writing is still bilinear, with limited development of ascenders; thin connecting strokes between letters within words are clearly visible; adjacent curves are often fused; and abbreviations remain numerous, drawn from the Gothic repertoire including the Tironian sign shaped like a "9" for the sequence con-.

Fig. 2 — Poggio, transitional script. Vatican, BAV, Pal. lat. 903. Image available through the Digital Vatican Library.

A mature example of Poggio's antiqua is seen in a manuscript of Cicero (Fig. 3). Its main characteristics are:

  • An airy and elegant page layout: ascenders are almost twice the height of the letter bodies; ample spacing between lines and words (though thin connecting strokes between letters within words can still be observed);
  • A limited use of abbreviations;
  • Carolingian-type letterforms: vertical d, vertical r, and vertical final s;
  • Two-lobe g connected by a straight stroke;
  • Preservation of the st ligature and reintroduction of the Carolingian ct ligature;
  • The revival of the Carolingian ampersand (&) for the sequence et;
  • Majuscules inspired by Roman capital letters.

Fig. 3 — Poggio, mature antiqua. Vatican, BAV, Vat. lat. 3245, fol. 1v. Image available through the Digital Vatican Library.

Compared to the late Carolingian minuscule of the eleventh century (Fig. 4), Poggio's antiqua differs in its more orderly mise en page, in the simplification of abbreviations, and in the survival of Gothic-derived feet linking letters on the baseline within words.

Fig. 4. Late Carolingian comparandum. Vatican, BAV, Vat. lat. 84. Image available through the Digital Vatican Library.

Niccolò Niccoli and Humanistic Cursive

Niccolò Niccoli (Florence, 1365–1437) was another key figure in the humanistic graphic reform. After coming into contact with the most refined intellectual circles of his time, he turned from commerce to the study of ancient books. Thanks to his friendship with Cosimo de' Medici, he assembled an extremely rich library, now part of the Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana in Florence. Niccoli is credited with the invention of humanistic cursive — also called all'antica — found primarily in manuscripts intended for his own private use. His autograph copy of Lucretius's De rerum natura is the principal witness.

Niccolò Niccoli, Lucretius' De rerum natura. Florence, Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Plut. 35.30. Image available from the Teca Digitale BML.

This script has its roots in mercantesca, the writing of Niccoli's early training, but tends toward antiqua, from which it borrows several features. It should not be understood as simply an antiqua written with a cursive ductus; it is an inherently cursive script, descending without interruption from the tradition of cursive and hybrid hands.

Features inherited from the Gothic cursive tradition include:

  • Single-compartment a;
  • f and s descending slightly below the baseline;
  • Final h with its second stroke descending below the baseline and forming a curve;
  • Frequent thin connecting strokes between letters.

Features derived from antiqua include:

  • An airy layout with spacing between lines, words, and sometimes letters;
  • Vertical final s;
  • Vertical d;
  • The ampersand (&) for et;
  • The ct ligature;
  • Majuscules inspired by Roman capitals.

Niccoli's personal contribution lies in: the ct ligature executed with a left-facing loop shaped like a flag; and the treatment of ascender tips, which are sometimes bent to the right, sometimes shaped like a spatula, and sometimes provided with small loops.

Humanistic Scripts beyond Poggio and Niccoli

In the first half of the fifteenth century, other scribes also devoted themselves to graphic experimentation inspired by ancient models. Antonio di Mario, for example, proposed a form of antiqua different from Poggio's, characterized by a thin and delicate stroke.

Antonio di Mario. Vatican, BAV, Vat. lat. 1969. Image available from the Digital Vatican Library.

With the recovery of Greek culture, some humanists integrated Greek characters into their scripts or composed entire manuscripts in Greek. Among them were Guarino of Verona and Ciriaco of Ancona (1391–1452), a merchant with a passion for archaeology. Inspired by the Greek inscriptions he observed during his travels, Ciriaco developed highly personal and fanciful scripts.

Ciriaco of Ancona. Vatican, BAV, Vat. lat. 10672. Image available from the Digital Vatican Library.

In the second half of the century, manuscript production in antiqua increased, partly because of the cultural projects of Italian rulers and popes who commissioned court scribes to assemble rich personal libraries (Milan, Mantua, Ferrara, Cesena, Rome, Naples, Urbino). From a graphic standpoint, scripts tended toward uniformity and standardization. In the final decades of the century, this writing was also influenced by the advent of printing.

The Development of Italic

In the evolution of humanistic cursive in the second half of the fifteenth century, the contributions of Antonio Sinibaldi and Bartolomeo Sanvito were particularly important.

Vatican, BAV, Vat. gr. 1626. Image available from the Digital Vatican Library.

Their scripts represent the first examples of the Italian chancery or Italic hand. Despite its cursive origins, this writing was mostly executed with an interrupted ductus, giving it an extremely calligraphic appearance. Italic became the preferred subject of study and debate among the most important sixteenth-century calligraphers, who established strict rules for its execution and developed various stylizations.

The calligraphic manual of Ludovico Vicentino (1522-1524). Image available through the Internet Archive.

Over the course of the sixteenth century, Italic established itself as a fundamental writing system, gradually replacing other script types and giving rise to the modern European cursive hands that descend to the present day.
