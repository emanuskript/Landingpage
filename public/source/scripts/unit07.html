Origins and Historical Context

Visigothic designates several script styles practiced on the Iberian Peninsula between about 700 CE and 1200 CE. The term is conventional in palaeography, although the Visigothic political period in Iberian history had effectively ended before the first extant codices begin appearing in the first half of the eighth century. Nevertheless, the script may have origins around 600 CE, over a hundred years before the first codices (Alturo i Perucho). Altogether, some 400 codices survive alongside thousands of charters. The exact lines of evolution are unclear, but the several styles probably had local origins in the new Roman cursive practiced in Iberia and may represent independent developments at different geographic and institutional centers.

Similarities to the Merovingian documentary hand may point to influence from northern Spain and across the Pyrenees (Alturo i Perucho). Similarities to Latin scripts used at the Monastery of St. Catherine on Mount Sinai led Bischoff to conjecture a possible African component in the development of the Visigothic book hand.

The Scripts

A cursive script served primarily for documentary writing; a minuscule script, which reduced the degree of ligature, served as the literary hand for book production. All these scripts evolved over time and were written, moreover, in informal and calligraphic registers. The establishment of the Umayyad Caliphate in 715 CE, which partitioned the peninsula, led to two distinctive cursive hands: Leonese, practiced in the Christian north; and Mozarabic, practiced by Latin writers living under the Umayyad Caliphate. Though minuscule and cursive were probably independent creations, features of both appear in a semi-cursive form.

The earliest surviving examples of the Visigothic book hand are: Verona, Biblioteca Capitolare, LXXXIX (84); and Autun, Bibliothèque Municipale, S 29 (27), fols. 63–76 (CLA 6.728, with a fragment in Paris, BNF, NAL 1628). Both date to the early eighth century.

The Calligraphic Book Hand

A fine calligraphic execution of the book hand is evident in the work of the scribe Florencio de Valeránica, in an early accomplishment: a copy of Gregory the Great's Moralia in Job (Commentary on the Book of Job), completed in 945 CE. The script leans to the left or is upright. The ascenders of b, d, h, and l show clubbingA characteristic widening at the top of ascender strokes, formed by a short approach stroke from the left before the main downstroke. Clubbing is a marker of several early medieval scripts..

Two Comparative Examples

Two samples of Visigothic book hand allow comparison of an early and a later phase:

Isidore's Etymologies, copied between 780 and 820. Madrid, Biblioteca Nacional de España, VITR/14/3, fol. 3v. Image from the BNE Digital Library.

Isidore's Etymologies, copied during the eleventh century. Madrid, Biblioteca Nacional de España, MSS/10008, fol. 6r-6v. Image from the BNE Digital Library.

Both codices transmit the Etymologiae of Isidore of Seville (d. 636), a standard reference work of the Middle Ages. The chapter De litteris latinis recounts the mythological origin of the Latin letters by the nymph Carmenta, mother of Evander, and is followed by a classification of letters into vowels and consonants.

The two samples differ markedly. The earlier example tends toward finer, more elongated letterforms with compressed spacing and shallow arches on h, m, and n. The later example shows heavier clubbing, more pronounced approach strokes on ascenders, clearer word separation, and more stylized abbreviations.
