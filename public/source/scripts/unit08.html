📜 Key Takeaways

  • Beneventan script was used principally in southern Italy from the late eighth century to the thirteenth century, and sporadically until the sixteenth.
  • Its calligraphic apogee occurred during the abbacies of Desiderius and Oderisius I at Monte Cassino (c. 1050–1105).
  • Three features define its aesthetic ideal: the broken minim; the horizontal ligature register at minim height; and contraposed curves in biting or fusion.
  • Of about 2,000 identified Beneventan items, nearly 75 percent contain liturgical texts.

Monte Cassino: History and Cultural Context

Among all the monasteries founded under the influence of Benedict of Nursia, the most celebrated was the abbey of Monte Cassino. This monastery was founded in the sixth century, perhaps 529 CE, between Rome and Naples on the site of an earlier pagan temple.

Benedict imposed his monastic rule at Monte Cassino, and the abbey still claimed to possess a copy of the Rule in Benedict's own hand as late as 896 CE, when that codex perished in a fire. Beginning in the late eighth century, the monastery developed a distinctive script, now named Beneventan after the Lombard duchy that in 744 CE endowed Monte Cassino with a vast territory of landed holdings.

The abbey's history is interrupted by three prolonged periods of vacancy. The first ran from 581 to 717 CE, when Lombard invasions forced the monks to abandon the monastery. Around 660 CE, during this absence, the body of Benedict — the center of Monte Cassino's cult — was taken to the abbey of Fleury in Francia. The second vacancy ran from 883 to 950 CE, when raiders forced the monks to retreat again; much of the ancient library was probably destroyed in these two disruptions, though a copy of Ambrosiaster's commentary on the Pauline Epistles (Montecassino, 150, pp. 65–910), dating before 570 CE, may preserve a survival of the old library's holdings, if it was not acquired after 950. A third vacancy ran from 1239 to 1266, when the abbey was occupied by forces of Emperor Frederick II and his son Manfred. Between the second and third vacancies — most particularly between c. 1050 and c. 1200 — the abbey achieved its apogee as a book-producing center.

Montecassino lay at a remarkable cultural crossroads. In the fifth century, southern Italy had sheltered North African Christians fleeing the Vandal regime and "heterodox" Christian intellectuals such as the Pelagian Julian of Eclanum, who brought their books. The abbey's restoration after 950 CE probably entailed a deliberate search for manuscripts in hidden book collections of southern Italy, which accounts for the unexpected appearance of works such as Varro's De lingua latina and a new copy of Vitruvius in the eleventh century (Newton, Scriptorium and Library at Monte Cassino, 321–323).

From the tenth century on, the abbey had ties to the medical school at Salerno, to the Byzantine world, and to the Arabic-speaking world of North Africa. Constantine the African, who hailed from North Africa and translated a major corpus of medical texts from Arabic into Latin, ultimately settled at Monte Cassino. The abbots Desiderius (1058–1087, later Pope Victor III) and Oderisius I (1087–1105) oversaw exceptionally productive scriptoria.

The Script and Its Range

Beneventan script emerged in the late eighth century, reached its apogee in the eleventh and twelfth centuries, and was gradually replaced by Gothic scripts in the thirteenth century, around the time of the monks' third exile. It was used principally in southern Italy, but also extended along the Dalmatian coast where Monte Cassino exercised a direct influence. It was preeminently a liturgical script and continued to be used in some religious houses until the sixteenth century.

Medieval scribes seem to have adopted the implicit convention of transcribing Italian-language works in Caroline or, later, Gothic scripts. One significant exception is the ninety-six verse lines of the Ritmo cassinese, added around 1200 to the end of an eleventh-century Latin Bible (Montecassino, MS 552). This is one of the earliest surviving texts written in the Italian language and the only Italian text copied in Beneventan script. Beneventan script served, in this way, as one of the earliest vehicles of written Italian.

Of the some 2,000 Beneventan items identified, almost 75 percent contain liturgical texts. The apogee of Beneventan aesthetic achievement falls in the abbacies of Desiderius (abbot 1058–1087) and Oderisius I (abbot 1087-1105) at Monte Cassino.

Three Defining Features

Three features distinguish the calligraphic ideal of Beneventan script:

  1. The broken minim. The short i-stroke extending from the midline to the baseline is not executed in a single stroke. A diagonal stroke is stopped, the pen is repositioned, and the stroke is continued as the bottom of the letter, forming two lozenge shapes. Letters built on the minim reveal the same stroke construction: i, m, n, u, h.
  2. The horizontal ligature register. The horizontal strokes of e, f, g, r, and t are joined to the following letter, creating a continuous horizontal register along the minim height. This is visible in a sequence such as regeret.
  3. Contraposed curves in biting or fusion. When a right-facing convex curve (as in b or o) meets a left-facing convex curve (as in c or e), the two strokes share a single line rather than overlapping. This produces a characteristic "bitten" or fused appearance.

Morphological Features and Ligatures

The morphological features and ligatures of Beneventan script betray its origins in new Roman cursive, probably in the form practiced in Italy in the seventh and eighth centuries. Many ligatures were possible. By the end of the ninth century, certain of them came to be considered "obligatory" (in E. A. Lowe's term): above all, this applies to i when it follows e, f, g, l, r, or t. From the late ninth or early tenth century, two ligatures for ti were distinguished depending on whether the sound was assibilated or not. The unassibiliated ligature comprised a t with an i descending below the baseline. The assibilated form had the vertically-turned t, somewhat like a figure 8, before the i. Optional ligatures (in Lowe's terminology) that might also appear include: ae, ci, ct, ec, et, mi, ni, nt, or, rit, rp, sp, st, sti, ta, te, tu, xp.

Punctuation

A distinctive system of punctuation developed within Beneventan script conventions. The full stop was indicated by two dots and a comma or by a raised dot. A middle stop (roughly equivalent to the English semicolon or period) was indicated by a point on the baseline. A weak stop (roughly equivalent to a comma) was marked by a point surmounted by a diagonal slash or by a simple slash. In the late tenth century, three dots in an inverted pyramid signified a question mark; in the following century, a superscript 2 came to signal the rising intonation of a question, sometimes paired with a mark signaling the expected answer.
