Unit II

Rustic Book Capitals

Home › Unit II

📜 Key Takeaways

  • Rustic capitals were the dominant calligraphic book script of Roman antiquity, used from the first century BCE through the sixth century CE.
  • Lateral compression and a strong heavy diagonal distinguish rustics from square capitals; the term "rustic" is conventional and does not reflect the script's actual use or prestige.
  • In the Middle Ages, rustic capitals survived primarily as a display script for titles and headings, ranking below square capitals in the hierarchy of display forms.

Form and Ductus

Here are two letter Os with a similar ductus.

Two letter Os compared.

Both are made in two top-to-bottom strokes that join at the lower right. Both show contrast — a balance of heavy and fine features — and the principles of construction and aesthetics between the two are similar. There is, however, one clear difference: the O on the right is narrower. Its interior space, or counterThe enclosed or partially enclosed space within a letter, such as the interior of an O or the bowl of a P., is less a circle than an almond. Where the left-hand O fits approximately within a square, the right-hand O fits better within a rectangle. It shows greater lateral compression.

The left-hand O is the square capital, treated in Unit I. The right-hand O is the so-called rustic capital. The term "rustic" is conventional in English-language palaeography, although it has no bearing on the script's usage or form. More precise terms — "canonical book capitals" (Cencetti, followed by Bischoff) — have not displaced it in English. Rustic book capitals were in fact the dominant calligraphic book script of Roman antiquity. A study of surviving examples identifies two phases: an early one from the first century BCE to the third century CE, and a later one from the fourth century through the sixth century CE.

Surviving Examples

Rustic capitals were used across the Roman world. The oldest substantial surviving example is a papyrus fragment discovered in Egypt that preserves the longest text we have from the hand of the Roman poet Cornelius GallusGaius Cornelius Gallus (c. 70–26 BCE), Roman poet and first prefect of Egypt. Long thought almost entirely lost; a papyrus fragment was discovered at Qasr Ibrim in 1978. (c. 70–26 BCE). The document may be contemporary with his life. Gallus moved from Rome to Egypt to serve as prefect of Alexandria and Egypt (30–27 BCE). The papyrus roll may have been produced in Egypt, or it may have arrived through commercial networks from Italy.

Its regular use declined in Latin after c. 100 CE. — a dot used to separate words — is a notable feature of early rustic manuscripts. It is mentioned by a number of Roman authors, including Seneca, and ceased to be used regularly after c. 100 CE (Ammirati).

Besides Egypt, early rustic capitals survive among the debris left by the eruption of Vesuvius in 79 CE at Herculaneum and Pompeii. The volcanic deposit preserved papyrus and even wood at Herculaneum. These papyrus scrolls continue to be examined, with new discoveries still emerging.

Vergil was ranked in Late Antiquity as the preeminent Latin classic. His works served to train students in writing, as papyrus fragments from the first century CE onward demonstrate. In one fragment a rustic capital is employed, but with a thinner-pointed pen, which did not permit the salient contrast of heavy and fine strokes characteristic of the calligraphic examples. Such fragments nonetheless illustrate the quick rustic script of an educated writer; some letter forms — notably A, B (with a left bow), and D — more closely resemble contemporary cursive forms.

School exercise based on Vergil, Aeneid, book 2, line 601. London, University College, Dept. of Greek and Latin, Papyrus 24. Available via Wikimedia Commons.

The artistry of the late fourth-century Vatican Vergil (Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana, Vat. lat. 3225), outfitted with some fifty surviving illustrations, has made it a milestone in late antique book history (Wright). The deployment of rustic capitals in a period when other book scripts were available probably signified Vergil's status as a classic text (Cavallo). Of around sixty surviving examples of rustic capitals from before c. 600, the overwhelming majority transmit non-Christian — that is, classical — authors. Two Christian poets, Prudentius and Sedulius, appear in rustics, and up to the year 600 merely two biblical fragments employ this script.

Letter Forms

In rustic capitals, the contrast of heavy and fine strokes is accentuated by a heavy diagonal sweeping from top left to bottom right. This recurrent diagonal determines the rhythm of the script. Combined with lateral compression, it gives the script a more rapid appearance — even if it remains a constructed or set script in which strokes are, as it were, painted with the same top-to-bottom and left-to-right ductus as square capitals. Even in early scripts, the arms of V can be broad (verging on a U-form) and the left arm may slope downward in the middle. In later book capitals, the vertical stroke — held nearly perpendicular to the baseline — tended also to be drawn with the full pen width, while curved strokes show smooth gradations between fine and heavy.

The letters can be imagined as written inside rectangles, sometimes overlapping one another to create a harmonious texture of ink on the surface. Some letters, such as N and H, tend toward a nearly square format. In the later phase, letters including N and O show greater compression. Some letters — L and F — may rise above the headline to distinguish them from I and E respectively.

The rustic forms are subtly weighted toward the bottom. The top of each letter tends to be narrower: this is visible in the upper arms of Y and in the small bows of B, P, and R. The bow of P is not invariably closed. These letters may also rise slightly above the headline. Cross-strokes resemble enlarged serifs; the narrow versions can become quite small, almost a point, especially in the later phase, when a number of cross-strokes tend to be shortened and horizontal midstrokes placed somewhat above the midpoint of the letter. Early A may have a crossbar; later A typically does not. The ends of vertical strokes (F, H, L, R, T) and diagonal strokes (A, M, N) are terminated with serifs, often slightly wavy. These serifs reinforce the baseline and stop the eye from moving down past it (Parkes). Other diagonals — on A and R — may receive a finish stroke where the pen lifts from the page. The bottom of letters tends to be heavier not only because of the serifs but because of a subtle thickening of the stroke, perhaps caused by slowing the pen or, more controversially, by increasing pressure on a flexible pen nib.

In the later phase of rustic capitals (fourth–sixth centuries), a few morphological changes emerge. G is finished by a long oblique tail rather than a hook. V broadens, resembling a U. The treatment of the upper right arm of H, already variable in the earlier phase, becomes an oblique extension of variable angle and expression, sometimes resembling a K — earning the description "K-shaped H."

Rustic Capitals as a Display Script

In the Middle Ages, rustic capitals became, like square capitals, a display script for titles, headings, and initials. Already in Pompeii, painted political notices (programmata) show the square capital placed above the rustic capital. Stone inscriptions elsewhere in the Roman Empire also display the square capital over the rustic form — which in epigraphy was called scriptura actuaria ("the writing of acts"). This configuration prefigures the later display hierarchy, in which square capitals typically ranked above rustic capitals.

The only sustained use of rustic capitals for an entire manuscript in the Middle Ages is found in the Leiden Aratea and the Utrecht Psalter (both ninth century). Whether the ninth-century designers of these manuscripts chose a throwback script or were copying Late Antique models written in rustics remains unclear.
