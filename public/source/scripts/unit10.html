📜 Key Takeaways

  • Gothic textualis, the principal formal book script of the later Middle Ages, intensifies features already present in pregothic Caroline: compression, chiaroscuro, and central visual weight.
  • A fundamental distinction separates northern textualis (narrow, angular, with hairline decoration and strong baseline definition) from southern textualis, known as Rotunda, which preserves the broad, rounded aspect of Caroline.
  • Within northern textualis, scholars distinguish subtypes according to the treatment of letter feet and the construction of the a: textus quadratus, textus praescissus, textus rotundus, and textus semiquadratus.
  • Early printers adopted textualis formata as their model; the Gutenberg Bible exemplifies this typographic realization.

From Caroline to Gothic

The principal Gothic book script, usually termed textualis formataThe most calligraphic grade of Gothic textualis, characterized by highly regular letterforms, systematic hairline decoration, and carefully controlled feet. Distinguished from the libraria (everyday book hand) and currens (rapid) grades., is generally understood as a development of pregothic Caroline minuscule. It intensifies features already present in the earlier script: increased density, stronger chiaroscuroThe contrast between heavy and fine strokes within a script. In Gothic textualis, the contrast is pronounced and systematic, contributing to the visual texture of the page., greater lateral compression, and enhanced central weight, accompanied by shallower descenders and lower ascenders.

Sample alphabet of pregothic Caroline minuscule.

Within textualis, a broad distinction may be made between northern and southern forms. Northern textualis is narrow and compressed, with letters occupying a predominantly rectangular or oval format. Southern textualis, known as Rotunda, incorporates certain elements of compression and angularity but preserves the open, broad, and rounded aspect of its Caroline foundation.

In northern Europe — particularly in northern France, the Low Countries, and England — Caroline minuscule begins showing signs of fundamental change in the later eleventh and twelfth centuries. A pregothic manuscript from Cîteaux, dating to the mid-twelfth century, already demonstrates an advanced stage in this transition.

Troyes, Bibl. Mun., 441, fol. 112v-113r, s. xii med. Image available via the IRHT.

The scribe strives for equal spacing between minim strokes; the feet of minims and the bases of descenders are finished with continuous hairline strokes; the ct ligature has disappeared; the oR ligature becomes more frequent; the ascenders of b, d, and l show broken, wedge-shaped clubbing.

Northern Textualis

Characteristic features of northern textualis include: frequent hairline strokes; triangular or quadrangular terminals at heads and feet; the verticalization of certain curved strokes (notably in a and e); and the angularization of others. Curved elements such as c and o may become angular; right-sided convex curves (as in b or o) overlap with mirrored convex curves (as in c or e), producing biting or fusionA characteristic feature of Gothic textualis in which two adjacent convex curves — for example, the right bow of b followed by the left bow of e — share a single stroke rather than occupying overlapping space. Fusion reduces the visual "waste" of adjacent curves and produces the dense, even texture that distinguishes textualis.. A 2-shaped r appears after right-sided convex curves.

Above all, the middle zone is graphically defined in the letter forms. Ascenders display broken tops; triangular or forked clubbing may occur at minim height (as in i, p, r, u, m, and n). Letters ending with a finishing stroke at the headline merge with an approach stroke at the same position — a principle that Zamponi calls "elision." Strokes ending on the baseline or in descenders may terminate in angled or curved feet. Descenders of g, j, p, q, and y, as well as the bow of h, may extend below the line; f and long s generally rest on the baseline.

Textus Quadratus

The most calligraphic form of northern textualis is textus quadratus, the principal variety of textualis formata.

Its defining feature is the systematic addition of quadrangular feet to minims and ascenders, and quadrangular heads to the tops of ascenders. An accomplished example is the pontifical of Renaud de Bar, bishop of Metz (1302–1316), which shows numerous fusions: de (as in cedens), ha, oR (including the -orum abbreviation), pe, and po. Hairline ornamentation is frequent, and the straight a (the "box a") is consistently employed.

The Pontifical of Renaud de Bar. Cambridge, Fitzwilliam Museum, 298, fol. 2v. Image available at the digital collection of the Fitzwilliam Museum.

One of the chief aspects of textualis formata is the changed ductus, now entailing a composition of each letter stroke by stroke. A rare witness to the ductus of textus quadratus survives in the writing manual of the scribe and writing master Bock, preserved at Yale's Beinecke Library. Each letter is analyzed by the sequence of its constituent strokes. The construction of b, h, k, and s shows that the shoulder-level spur was added as a final stroke. In one instance, Bock mistakenly executed the full letter o prematurely; the strokes were subsequently relabeled in their correct order.

Bock's writing manual from c. 1510–1517. Yale, Beinecke Library, MS 439. Image available at Yale Library Digital Collections.

A specimen of the complete alphabet in textualis formata (quadrata) is preserved in a fragment collected by the writer Samuel Pepys around 1700. The sample shows both forms of a: the double-bowed a and the box a.

Model alphabet from the Pepys collection fragment. Cambridge, Magdalene College, Pepys Library, PL 2981, p. 19, no. 56, fol. 1v. Image available in Fragmentarium.

Textus Rotundus (Northern)

The preservation of Caroline morphology in textualis is perhaps most visible in the form known as textus rotundus (northern). Despite its name, this script is not predominantly round; letters generally conform to a rectangular format, like other northern textualis types. It retains from its Caroline predecessor the rightward upward turn at the base of minims and ascender shafts — slanting feet that frequently touch the following letter. Its defining feature is the "box a," constructed of two minims joined at the top, bottom, and midpoint. The script also exhibits several -er fusions. Textus rotundus is not related to southern Rotunda (see below).

Textus Semiquadratus and Textus Praescissus

A specimen from the advertisement of a scribe named Hagen illustrates further varieties. The textus semiquadratus (Hagen's term) resembles textus rotundus except that its a is a double bow rather than a box. The textus praescissus — a name perhaps meaning "cut off in advance" — resembles textus quadratus but lacks the additional serifs at the baseline: minims and ascenders end bluntly on the line without feet.

Scribe Advertisement of Johann von Hagen. Berlin, Staatsbibliothek zu Berlin, Preußischer Kulturbesitz, Inventar-Nr. Ms. Lat. Fol. 384. Image available at Bildindex.

Southern Textualis: Rotunda

In southern Europe, particularly Italy, Gothic textualis — known as Rotunda — remained more closely aligned with Caroline minuscule. Despite its name, it bears no relation to the northern textus rotundus. Rotunda preserves the broad, rounded aspect of Caroline. A defining difference from northern textualis formata is the absence of pronounced feet at the baseline. Letters frequently terminate in a manner reminiscent of textus praescissus. As Derolez (103) observes, "neither the baseline nor the headline have the marked visual importance in Rotunda that they have in Northern Textualis."

Ascender tops and descender bases are typically undecorated. Several letters end flat on the baseline: f, the stem of h, the first two minims of m, the first minim of n, straight r, straight s, and usually the Tironian et. Decorative spurs and hairlines are rare. The minuscule a consists of a minim with a low bow, its top open or lightly closed with a hairline. Letters b, c, and e are markedly round; e is closed with a hairline tongue. Uncial d predominates before curved letters; straight d appears when the neighboring stroke of the next letter is vertical. The letter g is formed with a long curved stroke on the right extending from the middle into the lower zone. Straight r ends on the line; round r appears after bows. Straight s ends on the baseline with a shoulder-height spur, while round s is typical in final position; a narrow trailing s of two strokes may descend below the line. Curved u is common, whereas angular v is comparatively rare. Rotunda favors overlapping strokes rather than fused double strokes. Common combinations include bb, bc, bd, be, bo; dc, de, do (with uncial d); he, ho; oc, od, oe, og, oo, oq; pc, pe, pg, po, and pp. The only true ligature is st.

Petrarch owned and probably commissioned a copy of Vergil's works in Rotunda, now preserved as MS Milan, Biblioteca Ambrosiana, A 79 inf.

Petrarch's Vergil. Milan, Biblioteca Ambrosiana, A 79 inf, fol. 102r. Image available at Ambrosiana Digital Library.

Gothic Textualis and Early Printing

With the advent of printing in the mid-1450s, early typographers adopted textualis formata as their model. The first major production using movable type, the Gutenberg Bible (Mainz, c. 1455), exemplifies this choice. Typography reinforced the even and highly regulated execution that characterizes textualis formata above all other Gothic scripts. The Göttingen copy of the Bible — one of the few surviving vellum copies and the only complete example — illustrates the script's formal clarity in typographic form.

The Göttingen copy of the Gutenberg Bible. Göttingen, SUB, 2 BIBL I, 5955:1 INC RARA, fol. 1r. Image available via the Göttingen Digitization Centre.
