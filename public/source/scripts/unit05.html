Insular Scripts

Home › Unit V

📜 Key Takeaways

  • "Insular" designates the scripts of Ireland and the British Isles from the early seventh century to approximately the tenth century.
  • Julian Brown's classification distinguishes insular half-uncial (majuscule) from insular minuscule, the latter comprising three grades: set, cursive, and current.
  • Missionary activity brought insular scripts to the continent, where they influenced writing practices throughout the early medieval period.
  • Characteristic features include black ink, triangular wedge serifs, a diminuendo effect at line openings, and advanced use of word separation and punctuation.

Introduction: The Insular World

Insular is the collective term for the Celtic, Roman British, and Anglo-Saxon culture of Ireland and the British Isles, used in palaeography to describe scripts produced in those regions up to approximately the tenth century (M. Brown 2012, 121). England had been a Roman province until the gradual withdrawal of the Roman military, completed in 410 CE. Ireland, by contrast, had never been a Roman territory and thus occupied a unique position in the history of Latin script. It was Christianized during the fifth century, and the Latin Bible, liturgy, and texts that it received were written in a new language for its scribes. The expansion of monasteries in the sixth century and subsequent missionary activity on the continent meant that insular scripts influenced continental writing practices throughout the early medieval period (Bischoff 114): St. Columban worked in Italy, France, and Switzerland; St. Willibrord in the Netherlands and Luxembourg; St. Boniface in Germany (Stokes 12). In England, insular styles were supplanted by Caroline minuscule during the tenth century; they survived far longer in Ireland.

The Earliest Evidence

The earliest evidence of insular script may date to the early seventh century, represented by: the Codex Usserianus Primus (fragments of the four Gospels); the psalter known as the Cathach of Saint Columba (Dublin, Royal Irish Academy, MS 12 R 33); the Springmount Bog tablets, containing a fragment of the psalter; and fragments of Isidore's Etymologiae preserved in Saint Gall, Stiftsbibliothek, 1399 a 1. Together these witnesses show that in the seventh century a variety of scripts was available: the first two are majuscule half-uncial, the third a minuscule, the fourth a cursive.

The minuscule scripts apparently evolved from the insular half-uncial in response to the need for a faster, more cursive form of writing, achieved by using a narrower pen and slanting the writing angle (J. Brown, 63). Whether the insular half-uncial derived directly from half-uncial as practiced in Italy in the sixth century, or from an earlier phase of cursive half-uncial (also called quarter-uncial) to which it shows certain resemblances, remains under discussion.

Julian Brown's Classification

In the classification system of Julian Brown, insular script comprises a graded series of hands: insular half-uncial (sometimes called majuscule), insular hybrid minuscule, and three further variants of this hybrid minuscule called set minuscule, cursive, and current (J. Brown, 201ff.). The first three grades tend to appear in biblical and liturgical codices; the last two were used for glosses, commentaries, and non-religious, literary, and grammatical works (J. Brown, 202; Clemens/Graham, 139).

Insular Half-Uncial (Majuscule)

The insular half-uncial was the most formal of the insular scripts and was used for a number of famous Gospel books usually dated to the late seventh or eighth century: the Lindisfarne Gospels, the Book of Durrow, and the Book of Kells. Its use faded after the eighth century except as a display script; thereafter, the highest grade for biblical and liturgical texts was the hybrid minuscule.

The half-uncial was written with a broad pen held at a straight angle, so that vertical strokes are heavy and horizontal strokes are fine. It is predominantly a bilinear script, with letters fitted between a headline and a baseline. It makes prominent use of circular and oval forms.

Although mainly a half-uncial script, variant forms were available: uncial forms of F, R, and S appear regularly; D and N had both uncial and half-uncial forms; G was usually half-uncial but the uncial form was also available (J. Brown 63). Ligatures are formed on A, E, L, R, and T but do not conspicuously alter the letter shapes. The minims of i, m, n, and u are very straight and regular, with a thick and flat base (Stokes 4). Serifs shaped like triangular wedges appear at the tops of ascenders and minims; these seem to derive from approach strokes (J. Brown 63).

Insular Minuscule

The insular minuscule, in contrast, inclines slightly to the left and shows greater lateral compression, allowing more letters per line. The ascenders and descenders become longer and sharper; minims and descenders have pointed, sometimes curved, foot serifs that have been likened to "cat's claws" (Stokes 213). Ascenders still have triangular wedges at the tops. Specific letter forms include: a with both closed and open forms; uncial d, n, r, and s available as variants; f and s set low on the line; flat-topped g; p and q sometimes slightly pinched at the top; the shoulder of r dropping to the baseline and curving up again; the shaft of t resembling c; both branches of y curving to the right.

Within the minuscule, Julian Brown distinguishes three grades:

  • Set minuscule separates all strokes by penlifts.
  • Cursive reduces the number of penlifts in a, f, h, m, n, p, r, and s (J. Brown 201).
  • Current is written more quickly by linking letters and parts of letters, and draws on a wider set of abbreviations. In this grade, quite small and angular, the letters a, i, o, u, s, and t sometimes hang below the line when in ligature.

Codicological and Material Features

Graphic, material, and codicological features help distinguish insular productions from continental ones. The ink of insular codices tended to be quite black, and calf parchment was used (Bischoff 118). Quires of ten leaves (rather than eight) are common, and the quire was pricked in both margins and then ruled after folding (J. Brown 184).

One of the most distinctive features of insular script — probably reflecting the fact that Latin was a second language for its practitioners — is the advanced use of visual cues to aid reading: word separation, punctuation, and display letters drawn from square capitals and uncials (M. Brown 2012, 127). Moreover, the first several letters of a line might show a diminuendo effect: the initial letter is drawn at a larger scale and the following letters decrease progressively in size before reaching the normal modulus. Initials were often further decorated by an outline of red dots (Bischoff 118).
