Unit III

Old and New Roman Cursive

Home › Unit III

📜 Key Takeaways

  • Old Roman cursive (ORC) was the rapid everyday script of the Roman world; it differs from rustic book capitals chiefly in its reduction of strokes and in ligature.
  • New Roman cursive (NRC) emerged in the late third century CE and extended over a four-line scaffold with distinct upper, middle, and lower zones — a structural innovation with lasting consequences.
  • New Roman cursive was the common script of the Late Roman Empire and the direct ancestor of the regional minuscule scripts (Merovingian, Visigothic, Beneventan, and others) that emerged around 600 CE.

Old Roman Cursive

Look closely at this papyrus. It comes from a special discovery at Oxyrhynchus in Egypt. In 1896 British archaeologist began to excavate a massive rubbish heap at this Egyptian town, preserving in the arid conditions untold number of papyri, which are still being opened and read today. This particular papyrus is a declaration regarding a Roman census for the years 47–48 CE. The top and bottom texts use two different scripts.

Image available at PSI Online

The same abbreviation appears on the right side of each text. It stands for a unit of Roman currency, and here we find “400 sesterces.” In fact, the texts are the same, but the papyrus incurred material losses at different places, and the surviving text in each version can help fill the other’s gaps. Try to read the two texts together. Where do they match?

The two texts were probably by a single scribe in two different scripts. The script at the top is the rustic book capital. It is constructed by adding several individual elements to one another. The strokes of the elements meet at junctures. The cardinal rule of library ductusThe direction and sequence of pen strokes used to construct a letter. In book hands, the rule is top to bottom and left to right. Cursive scripts frequently deviate from this rule in order to maintain contact between letters. (i.e., the ductus related to a script intended for books) is that the pen should move from top to bottom and from left to right. The script on the bottom does not always obey that rule. It is a cursive capital script, the so-called old (or early) Roman cursive.

The script of the Roman world in everyday use — for letters, accounts, and documents — was the cursive capital script known today as old Roman cursive (ORC). According to Robertis, it differs from rustic book capitals above all in the reduction and simplification of letter elements: by lifting the pen less frequently from the page, the writer produces horizontal connecting strokes to the right. This is what makes a script cursive.

An even more rapid example is a speech by Emperor Claudius (41–54 CE), almost contemporary with that census.

Berlin, Staatliche Museen, P. 8507 R. Image available at the Berliner Papyrusdatenbank

The writing speed is accelerated by the reduction of defining letter elements. A notable feature of the ORC ligature system is the interpenetration of letter elements: letters may be connected by strokes moving to the right, and certain pairs — am, bu, em, en, er, es, ma, re, ur, us — could be written in ligature.

British Library, Papyrus 229 (sale of a slave, 166 CE). Image availabe from BL Digital Collections

The pen employed for ORC was narrower than that used for rustic book capitals, producing less stroke contrast. This is visible in B, H, P, Q, and R. Moreover, when the essential morphology of letters is similar, the reduction in the number of strokes is clear: compare E (reduced from four strokes to three), and S.

The next document, dating to 166 C.E., illuminates one of the realities of Roman society: the sale and purchase slaves. Here is the first sentence:

C(aius) Fabullius Macer, optio classis praetor(iae) Misenatium, (triere)
Tigride, emit puerum, natione transfluminianum,
nomine Abban quem et Eutychen, siue quo alio nomine
uocatur, annorum circiter septem, pretio denariorum
ducentorum.

C. Fabullius Macer, adjutant of the praetorian fleet of Misenum, of the trireme Tigris, buys a boy, of the people on the other side of the river, by the name of Abbas, also known as Eutyches, or by whatever other name he is called, around seven years old, for the price of two hundred denarii. (translation original)

Old Roman cursive is perhaps less a canonical model than a practice. At the bottom of the slave-sale document of 166 CE, five different Latin hands and one Greek hand sign the document, illustrating wide variation in the mastery of penmanship. The letters are similarly constructed around a diagonal stroke moving from top right to bottom left; this stroke creates the characteristic rhythm of the letter sequence and gives it an illegible aspect.

The Imperial Chancery and the Litterae Caelestes

During the second and third centuries CE, the script of imperial administration stabilized, as scribes favored the simplest letters and fastest ligatures. Amid the wide range of possibilities for ligature, one set of ligatures, allowing the most rapid motion between letters, became characteristic of the busy administrative offices of the Roman Empire, where rapid execution was important. By the last quarter of the third century, the conventionalization of letter forms and ligatures had produced an independent official script. By the fourth century, this script had probably become elaborately stylized, its unique features serving as graphic sources of imperial authority. A rescript — that is, a reissued law — of 367 CE reminds contravening provincial administrators that the imperial chancery reserved to itself the script called litterae caelestesLiterally "heavenly letters": the elaborate bureaucratic hand reserved by the imperial chancery as a graphic sign of imperial authority. The sole surviving example dates to the fifth century., the highly elaborated bureaucratic hand. The sole surviving example of this litterae caelestes dates to the fifth century (PLouvre 2404 + PLeiden 421, ChLA XVII 657).

PLouvre 2404 + PLeiden 421. Image in F. Steffens, Paléographie latine , plate 16.

The same law tells us that it had long been customary to privilege litterae caelestes as an imperial sign, and that provincial administrators had been unlawfully appropriating the style. They were instead supposed to use the litterae communes — the script in common use for everyday writing. This common script is the subject of the next section.

New Roman Cursive

The origins of new Roman cursive (NRC) have been much discussed. Its appearance in the late third century, after the decline of old Roman cursive, led earlier scholars to suppose it had evolved directly from the earlier administrative hand (Schiaparelli). A different view (Mallon) proposed that NRC developed within rustic book capitals: what changed were the material and technical conditions — the angle of pen and page, and the concomitant shift from scroll to codex. A third hypothesis (Cencetti, Casamassima/Staraz, Robertis) holds that NRC emerged from patterns of ligature and ductus that had become widespread in common writing outside both administrative and book contexts. The third hypothesis is the most widely accepted today.

NRC preserved from the earlier ductus a procedure for constructing ligatures and developed new graphic options for letters. Some letter variants were well adapted to ligature through a stroke — common in ORC — that moves first right and then downward: e, f, g, r, t. This cursive stroke was developed in a new form of ligature on e and t, in which two similar elements of different letters were executed in a single stroke — not by a supplementary connecting stroke, but by "superimposing" two similar elements as a single solution.

The Four-Line Scaffold

According to Robertis, the great structural innovation of new Roman cursive is the organization of the writing field. In the earlier book capitals, letters were written between an even headline and a baseline; verticals were of similar length. Old Roman cursive also extended mainly between a headline and baseline, making fast writing difficult to read. In new Roman cursive, by contrast, the verticals have different lengths. The script extends over a four-line scaffold articulating three zones — upper, middle, and lower:

  • Short strokes meet in the middle zone, defined by the rounding of vertical downstrokes as they approach the baseline (Robertis 66–67): t, e, l, a. This rounding enables a continuous flow into subsequent letter elements, as seen in a, d, g, q, and u.
  • The letters b, d, h, k, and l have ascenders extending into the upper zone; c, e, f, and s also have upper elements.
  • The letters f, g, p, and q have descenders into the lower zone; i, r, s, and x show some downward elaboration.

Ligatures in NRC normally took place from the top of the middle zone downward. Letters terminating in the upper zone, the lower zone, or on the baseline were not well suited to ligature. Since many letters now extended across three zones, many that had allowed ligatures in ORC now excluded them. Fewer variants were thus disposed to ligature with the next letter, and the script proceeds across the line as cursive sequences with stops determined by the terminal strokes of particular letters. Over time, the letters a, l, and sometimes c tended increasingly to form ligatures in an ascending motion from the baseline — a fresh cursive expansion that had been uncustomary in old Roman cursive. It is already visible in the names Achillio and Vitalis at the top of an early example.

Later Elaboration

From the late fifth century, the script was elaborated in the top zone — particularly in the top line of a writing space — with extended letters and decorative loops. These loops may define chancery styles and the tradition was preserved well into the Middle Ages.

A land grant made to the church of Ravenna in about 600 CE illustrates NRC in a formal documentary context. In this example, the upper zone is expanded and the middle zone compressed. The ascenders receive loops with approaching upswings.

Land grant to the church of Ravenna. Manchester, John Rylands Library, Latin MS 1: digitalcollections.manchester.ac.uk

Another trend was the expansion of the middle zone by distancing its bounding lines, thus increasing the relative lengths of the verticals and producing rounder letters. In the so-called Butini papyrus, this tendency is so marked that the letters have been termed litterae elongatae ("elongated letters"): downstrokes are vertical, stroke junctions are distant from the baseline, and ligatures move from the top (Robertis 72).

Butini papyrus: Geneva, Bibliothèque de Genève, Archives BGE, Ms. Lat. inv. 75 recto. Image from BGE digital archive

Although this document did not come from the imperial chancery itself, it was issued by a high imperial functionary of the mid-sixth century. It is now understood to be a calligraphic development of the common new Roman cursive, at a lower administrative level than the chancery. Its main graphic tendencies were preserved in Merovingian charter scripts (Mallon).

In sum, new Roman cursive shows great variety. As the documentary and everyday script of the Late Roman Empire, it was the direct ancestor of the regional scripts that emerged around 600 CE. The similarities and divergences between those successor scripts often find their explanation at this earlier level.
