Unit I Writing Supports

📜 Key Takeaways

  • Medieval writing supports include papyrus, parchment, wax tablets, and paper — each with distinct properties and historical ranges.
  • Papyrus dominated antiquity; parchment dominated the European Middle Ages; paper spread from the late thirteenth century onward and became dominant before the printing press.
  • Palimpsests — reused parchment — can be recovered by modern imaging technologies such as multispectral imaging.

Throughout much of antiquity, the primary writing support for books was papyrus. Manufactured in Egypt from the pith of the papyrus plant (cyperus papyrus), it was widely used across the Greek and Roman world. Thin strips of the plant were laid in perpendicular layers, pressed, and dried to form sheets with distinctive horizontal and vertical grains. These sheets were then pasted together along the edges and rolled into scrolls, as in the picture below. In antiquity, such papyrus rolls were transported in cylindrical containers or baskets.

A man holding a papyrus scroll. Before 79 C.E. National Archaeological Museum of Naples (inv. nr. 9072). Public domain via Wikimedia Commons.

For Latin literature, the oldest surviving poetry — that of Cornelius GallusGaius Cornelius Gallus (c. 70–26 B.C.E.), Roman poet and soldier. Long considered almost entirely lost; a papyrus fragment was discovered at Qasr Ibrim in 1978. — survives on a papyrus fragment discovered in Egypt. Yet the most famous surviving examples come from the Villa of the Papyri in Herculaneum, preserved under volcanic ash during the eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 C.E. These carbonized rolls are extremely fragile, and for centuries their contents remained largely inaccessible. Recent imaging technologies, including advanced scanning and virtual unrolling techniques, now allow scholars to read portions of these texts without physically opening them. Among the most exciting developments is the possible identification of historical works attributed to Seneca the ElderMarcus Annaeus Seneca (c. 54 B.C.E.–39 C.E.), Roman rhetorician and father of the philosopher Seneca the Younger. His Controversiae and Suasoriae are the main surviving works., whose lost historical writings may survive within these damaged scrolls.

Although papyrus is often considered too brittle for codex construction, there is evidence that papyrus codices did exist. A remarkable discovery at the University Library of Graz in 2023 identified a papyrus fragment that had originally been folded and bound as a bifolium within a codex structure. If this interpretation is accepted, it represents the oldest surviving bifolium from antiquity, demonstrating that the codexA book formed from bound stacked sheets, as opposed to the scroll/roll (volumen). The codex gradually replaced the roll from the second century C.E. onward. Its advantages include random-access reading, use of both sides of each leaf, and greater compactness. form — later dominant in medieval manuscripts and modern books — was already being explored while papyrus was still in use. While papyrus codices in the European Middle Ages are rare, famous examples survive in the fragmentary remains of the homilies of Avitus of VienneAlcimus Ecdicius Avitus (c. 450–518 C.E.), bishop of Vienne, Gaul. His works include biblical epic poetry and theological letters. and in the Antiquitates Iudaicae (Antiquities of the Jews) of Flavius JosephusFlavius Josephus (c. 37–c. 100 C.E.), a Roman-Jewish historian. His 20-volume work Antiquities of the Jews (93 AD), traces the history, laws and customs of the Jewish people from creation to the first century. It was translated into Latin by Rufinus of Aquileia (c. 345–411 C.E.), both of the sixth or seventh century.

After papyrus fell out of use in European book production, parchmentA writing material made from the skin of a calf (vellum), sheep, or goat, prepared by de-hairing, liming, stretching under tension on a frame, and scraping with a lunellum. Unlike leather, parchment is not tanned but dried under tension. The word derives from Pergamum (modern Bergama, Turkey). became the dominant writing support in Europe until at least 1300. Pliny famously remarked that "the immortality of all human beings depends on it." While papyrus continued in widespread use for centuries, parchment ultimately proved more durable in medieval Europe and adaptable for the codex format.

Yet the account is more legend than history: parchment long predates the episode described by Pliny. For writing on treated animal skin was known in the Near East well before the Hellenistic period. In the Middle Ages, the material for parchment was derived from the dermis layer of calfskin, sheepskin, or goatskin. The Roman poet MartialMarcus Valerius Martialis (c. 38/41–102/104 C.E.), Roman poet famous for his epigrams. gives one of the earliest references to a parchment codex, and the earliest surviving parchment fragment with Latin script, known as De bellis macedonicis, dates to around 100 C.E. Modern science has opened new avenues for studying parchment through Zooarchaeology by Mass Spectrometry (ZooMS)A biomolecular technique that identifies animal species from collagen peptide markers using mass spectrometry. Developed in the early 2000s and first applied systematically to manuscripts c. 2009.. By analyzing the collagen profile of tiny samples, even those collected with a PVC eraser, researchers can identify the animal species used.

Parchment roduction begins with soaking the raw pelt of animal to remove blood and impurities, followed by liming, which involves submerging the skin in an alkaline solution of slaked lime for anywhere from three to ten days. This causes the collagen fibers to swell and burst their casings, it loosens the hair and follicles, and it strips away natural grease. Perhaps transmitted from the Islamic world, the technique of liming is attested in European literary sources by c. 800 CE and marked a departure from the acidic vegetable infusions used in antiquity. After liming, the pelt is unhaired by scraping on a slanted board, fleshed to remove fat and muscle, trimmed of unusable sections, and then delimed in diluted vinegar to reduce the pH. Supplementary treatments such as bating (smearing in bird manure), puering (smearing in dog feces), or drenching with acids (barley, oat, ash bark, stale beer or urine) may also be applied. These procedures free remaining hair roots and grease, improve flexibility, and neutralize residual lime.

Preparation of parchment: stretching a skin on a frame as the parchment-maker scrapes with a lunellum. Public domain via Wikimedia Commons.

At this stage, the prepared skin could be finished in one of several distinct materials. Alum-tawed leatherLeather prepared by treating a de-haired skin with alum (potassium aluminium sulfate) and salt, then working and drying it. The result is white, firm, and supple. Unlike vegetable-tanned leather, alum-tawing does not chemically transform the collagen and can deteriorate under damp conditions. was treated with a potassium aluminium salt solution, then dried, and staked by stretching over a wooden surface to produce a characteristically white, supple material. Vegetable-tanned leatherLeather made by immersing a de-haired skin in a tannin-rich solution (typically from oak bark or oak galls) for weeks or months. The tannins bond with collagen to produce a durable, water-resistant material. The standard covering leather for medieval bookbinding., by contrast, was steeped for up to a year in a solution rich in tannins, particularly from oak bark and oak galls, which bonded with the collagen fibers to produce a durable material resistant to water, heat, and decay.

What fundamentally distinguishes parchment from leather is the drying process. After deliming, the wet, elastic skin is stretched tightly onto a wooden frame and dried under sustained tension. As it dries, the parchmentmaker scrapes the surface with a curved blade called a lunellumA crescent-shaped scraping tool used by the parchment-maker to thin and smooth the stretched skin as it dried. The scraping action, combined with the tension of the frame, reorganizes the collagen fibres into parallel layers, giving parchment its tautness and translucency., sometimes supplemented by pumice. This action, combined with the mechanical stress of stretching, reorganizes the normally irregular mesh of dermal collagen into uniform, parallel layers, which give parchment its characteristic tautness and translucency. Defects in parchment serve as material witnesses to both the skill of the maker and the life history of the animal. Veining results from inadequately drained blood, scarring from healed wounds, and holes from over-scraping, while cockling, a warping or puckering of the sheet, stems from uneven drying or later exposure to humidity.

The investment of labor and capital in parchment making meant that parchments were reused as palimpsests. In palimpsests, older texts on the writing support were scraped or washed off so that the surface could be written on again. Though the original writing was often faintly visible, modern imaging technologies such as multispectral imaging (MSI)A technique that captures images at multiple wavelengths of light (visible, infrared, ultraviolet), revealing materials invisible to the naked eye — used extensively in manuscript studies to recover erased or faded text. now enable scholars to recover many erased texts.

A fundamental writing support in antiquity and the Middle Ages was the wax tablet. A wooden tablet was recessed and filled with wax, into which letters were inscribed using a stylus. The writing could be erased by smoothing the wax surface with a spatula, a flat-edged tool sometimes at the opposite end of a stylus, like a modern pencil eraser. Wax tablets were thus ideal for ephemeral texts, such as notes, drafts and school exercises, of which some examples survive. Multiple tablets could be hinged together to form a diptych or polyptych, an early precursor to the codex.

From the early centuries C.E. onward, paper gradually emerged as another major writing support. Invented in China by the second century B.C.E., paper spread westward through Islamic lands by the eighth century and reached parts of Europe by the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. Paper was produced by macerating plant fibers — including linen rags, hemp, bark, or other organic material — in water to create pulp. This pulp was scooped onto a mesh screen, forming a thin mat as water drained away, then pressed and dried into sheets. Compared to parchment, paper was cheaper and faster to produce, though generally less durable. Well before the spread of the printing press after the mid-fifteenth century, paper was becoming the dominant support for books in Europe.

Unit II Page Folding & Formats

📜 Key Takeaways

  • Parchment sheets were folded to produce standard formats: folded once = folio (2 leaves); twice = quarto (4 leaves); three times = octavo (8 leaves).
  • The bifolium — one sheet folded in half — is the most common building block of a quire.
  • A quarto quire of eight leaves typically required two parchment skins; an octavo quire could be formed from a single sheet.

The primary evidence for the history of medieval book production is the surviving codices and fragments themselves. In addition to this, there are recipes and craft guides, which mostly stem from the late Middle Ages. A third source is iconography – illustrations that contains books or, in some cases, show stages in the book-making process. Perhaps the most detailed illustration is an image added to the beginning of a late twelfth-century manuscript containing works by the church father Ambrose (c. 339-397).

Illustration of the creation of a book (c. 1200). Bamberg, Staatsbibliothek, Msc.Patr.5. Digital image provided by the digital holdings of the Bamberg Staatsbibliothek.

In eleven medallions it shows key steps in the process:
1. Drafting on wax tablet
2. Producing parchment
3. Cutting and ruling the leaves
4. Sharpening the quill
5. Writing by a scribe
6. Illustrating or decorating
7. Cutting the wooden book cover
8. Fashioning the metal parts
9. Binding the codex on a sewing frame
10. Displaying the completed codex
11. Using the codex to teach
We are moving from the second to the third step, from producing parchment to folding a quire.

After the pelt has been turned into parchment it was trimmed. A vivid illustration of the trimmed sheet comes from a visionary priest of the fourteenth century, Opicinus de CanistrisOpicinus de Canistris (1296–c. 1352), Pavian cleric and mapmaker at the Avignon papacy. His large-format cosmographic diagrams on unfolded parchment sheets survive in the Vatican Library (Pal. lat. 1993 and Vat. lat. 6435)..

Drawing on full parchment sheet by Opicinus de Canistris. Available via the digital collection of the Biblioteca Apostolica di Vaticana.

To record his visions of the world and the end times, he procured twenty-seven parchment sheets. Differences in thickness at the tail and neck, variations in grain, and the location of follicle clusters near limbs can be detected in parchment sheets like this one. As Opicinus did not assemble these sheets into a codex, his collection demonstrates the scale at which parchment was produced and sold.

The trimmed parchment sheet was cut and folded into a number of leaves, which were then assembled into quires. A single sheet could produce a folio, quarto, or octavo formats, depending on the number of folds. In principle, a parchment sheet could be used to produce pages of any of these formats, although the actual size of the page depended on the dimensions of the animal from which the parchment was made. For example, a quire in quarto format typically required two parchment skins, while an octavo quire could be formed from a single sheet folded three times to produce eight leaves. If the full parchment sheet was folded once, it created two leaves at folio size; if it was folded twice, it produced four quarto leaves; if it was folded three times, it produced eight octavo leaves. A single sheet of parchment folded in half to form two leaves is a bifoliumA single sheet folded once to create two leaves (four pages). Nested bifolia form a quire. The bifolium is sewn through its central fold during binding.. The bifolium is the most common building block of a quire.

Unit III Making & Ruling Quires

📜 Key Takeaways

  • Despite considerable variety, quires were widely in quaternions composed of four bifolia (eight leaves) with the hair side of skin facing the hair side and the flesh side facing the flesh side — "Gregory's Rule."
  • Quires were pricked and ruled before writing to establish text lines, margins, and page layout (mise-en-page).
  • Tacketing (a temporary thread or parchment-strip fastening) held quires together during handling before final binding.
  • Ruling techniques (drypoint, lead point, ink) varied by period and scriptorium tradition.
  • Quire signatures, catchwords, and (from the 14th century) foliation helped maintain the correct sequence of leaves and gatherings.

A quireAlso called a gathering or fascicle. A set of nested bifolia (folded sheets) sewn together through their central fold. The standard medieval quire contained four bifolia (eight leaves, sixteen pages), though quires of two, three, five, or more bifolia also occur. is a stack of collected parchment sheets that would form a codex. The building block was the bifolium — a sheet that was folded into two folios (or four pages), but single leaves ("singletons") also appear. The most common format was the quaternion, which consisted of four nested bifolia. Of course, gatherings might also consist of two bifolia (binions), three bifolia (ternions), five bifolia (quinions), or something else.

Usually, the hair side, often somewhat darker, was on the outside, and facing pages matched one another, hair with hair, flesh with flesh. Once stacked into a quire and a layout scheme was determined, they were pricked with an awl or blade, and then lightly scored, or ruled, to furnish guidelines for copying the text. Larger scriptoria employed teams of scribes, rubricators, and illuminators working in a coordinated sequence., a quire has fallen to the floor, but the pages stay together because it has been tacketedTacketing: a method of temporarily securing a folded quire by passing a thread or strip of parchment through small holes near the top of the central fold, forming a simple staple-like fastening. Sometimes left in place when the book was bound., or joined by a fastening thread or parchment strip near the top, like a modern staple or paperclip.

Collation refers to the arrangement of leaves – how the quires and leaves have been folded and stacked – within a manuscript. Leaves were typically stacked so that hair sides faced hair sides and flesh sides faced flesh sides. This aesthetic arrangement, first noted by New Testament scholar Caspar René GregoryCaspar René Gregory (1846–1917), American-German New Testament scholar known for his systematic analysis of Greek manuscripts of the New Testament and the establishment of the "Gregory-Aland" manuscript numbering system. He first articulated "Gregory's Rule" concerning parchment orientation. (1846–1917), is known as "Gregory's Rule"The observation, first systematized by New Testament scholar Caspar René Gregory (1846–1917), that in medieval parchment manuscripts hair sides face hair sides and flesh sides face flesh sides across each opening. and is preserved across folio and quarto formats as well. If a manuscript is not consistent with this rule, it may be a sign that leaves have been lost or misbound.

The aesthetic effect produced by this hair-flesh patterning may also be the result of the folding technique used in making a quire. In most common techniques of folding a large parchment sheet, such as the octavo format, the hair side faced hair side, and flesh side faced flesh side. When the folds along the edges were cut, the resulting quire naturally paired facing hair sides and facing flesh sides together. If most medieval manuscripts hold true to this rule, they also place the hair side on the outside of the quire. In contrast, late antique codices, insular ones and those from specific medieval scriptoria like the abbey of CorbieCorbie Abbey (founded c. 657 C.E.), near Amiens in northern France. A major early medieval scriptorium responsible for important developments in Caroline minuscule script. tended to place the flesh side on the outside of the quire.

Once the quires were stacked, they could be pricked and ruled in preparation for writing. Pricking involved making small punctures or slits along the edges of the leaves to mark the positions of lines and margins. Ruling established the guidelines for text lines, margins, and other elements of the page layout (mise-en-pageFrench term for the overall layout of a manuscript page: the arrangement of text columns, margins, ruling lines, initials, and decorative elements. Different periods and scriptoria developed characteristic mise-en-page conventions that can help date and localize a manuscript.). These lines could be incised with a stylus, awl, or penknife (scalpellum) — all forms of drypoint ruling -, drawn with a lead point, or, more often in the late Middle Ages, applied in ink. The visible lines of lead point and especially of ink turned ruling into a part of the aesthetic of the page. There is no general typology of ruling patterns, but particular techniques may reflect house or local practices.

The stylus, the awl, and the penknife probably served for pricking and ruling. Dividers — the compass or calipers (Latin circinus) — could also be used for marking curves in diagrams and illustrations. They were, for example, needed for drawing the geometric diagrams that fill many medieval codices, such as those described by Isidore of SevilleIsidore of Seville (c. 560–636 C.E.), bishop and encyclopedist, author of the Etymologiae, a major reference work of the early Middle Ages. (c. 560–636 C.E.) in the so-called Liber rotarum (Book of Wheels), a treatise about the nature of the world featuring a series of diagrams made with circles.

Before the scribe began writing, the folded sheets of a quire were often kept together by tacketing. Small holes were pierced near the top of the quire's central fold, and a thread or twisted strip of parchment was drawn through and tied, forming a simple staple-like binding. Unit IV Scribal Tools & Techniques

📜 Key Takeaways

  • The scribe's primary tools were the quill and the penknife (scalpellum); the reed pen (calamus) was standard in antiquity but largely replaced in medieval Europe.
  • Scribes first learned to write on wax tablets with a metal or ivory stylus; drypoint tools remained in use in the scriptorium for ruling and annotation.
  • The traditional three-finger grip differs from the modern school grip and involved the middle finger for fine control.
  • Parchment was dressed with calcium-based powders to remove grease and improve ink adhesion before writing began.
  • Reflectance Transformation Imaging (RTI) can reveal faint drypoint inscriptions and scribal markings invisible to the naked eye.

Once the quire was prepared for copying, the scribe went to work. He or she held a quill pen in one hand and a penknife in the other for scraping mistakes away and sharpening the pen. The writing desk was equipped with an inkpot or inkhorn for black ink.

In Antiquity and the Middle Ages, people first learned to write on wax tablets with a drypoint stylus. Most styluses were probably made of metal, though ivory examples also survive. Literary sources from Late Antiquity and the early Middle Ages preserve vivid — and sometimes violent — classroom anecdotes. One legend claims that the ninth-century scholar John Scottus EriugenaJohannes Scottus Eriugena (c. 800–877 C.E.), Irish philosopher and theologian working at the Carolingian court of Charles the Bald. His major work is the Periphyseon (On the Division of Nature).(c. 800–877 C.E.) was killed by students armed with their styluses. Though apocryphal, it captures something of the intensity and discipline associated with early medieval schooling. Styluses preserved in ancient graves show traces of ink on the tip, suggesting that they were also used on papyrus and perhaps parchment supports. Furthermore, a large body of understudied texts contain drypoint scribbles, probably made with a stylus. These were users' comments and often belonged learners who had not yet graduated to the quill pen. Today, the faint drypoint words, lines and impressions left by stylus or compass on parchment can be studied using Reflectance Transformation Imaging (RTI)A computational photographic technique that captures surface shape and color using images taken from multiple lighting angles, enabling visualization of subtle surface relief invisible to the naked eye. Widely used in manuscript and archaeological studies., which reveals subtle incisions invisible to the naked eye.

When it came to writing with ink, the reed pen (calamus) was standard in Antiquity. Though largely replaced in medieval Europe, it makes a memorable appearance in a riddle from the Exeter BookA manuscript anthology of Old English poetry, donated to Exeter Cathedral by Bishop Leofric (d. 1072 C.E.). While there is little evidence for pen grips, the most common manner of depiction, such as this one of Marie de France, shows the scribe with the middle and index fingers on top of the shaft of the quill and the thumb underneath. This "three-finger pose" contrasts with the modern school grip that place only the index finger on top; this latter grip is infrequently represented in medieval imagery. The traditional posture is echoed in the well-known scribal saying: Tres digiti scribunt, totum corpus laborat — "Three fingers write, the whole body labors." While, moreover, modern writers tend to write by the coordinated motion of the fingers and the rotation of the wrist from the elbow, medieval writers probaby wrote in a more "painter"-like way by moving the whole arm from the elbow, reserving fine and detailed work to the action of the middle finger controlling the pen tip.

Before writing, scribes might prepare the page for writing. Parchment could receive several treatments or dressings that affected the fineness of the leaf and the ability of ink to adhere in a uniform way. Some of these processes may have been done not by the parchment-maker, but by the scribe. Parchment could be smoothed with pumice or cuttlebone. It could be dressed in calcium-based powders, like pounceA fine powder (often made from cuttlefish bone, sandarac resin, or pumice) dusted onto parchment or paper to reduce grease and prevent ink from spreading. Also used after writing to absorb excess ink., gesso (a thin calcium paste), powdered quicklime (made from kiln-heating limestone/ seashells), bone, chalk, egg shell. These substances removed grease — especially from the flesh side — and prevented ink from running. Recipes describe finely ground substances applied and then rubbed in, sometimes with a rabbit's foot kept for that purpose.

Unit V Ink, Pigments, & Page Decoration

📜 Key Takeaways

  • Medieval inks fall into three main types: carbon ink; iron-gall ink; and vegetable-based or mixed inks. Iron-gall ink was dominant throughout most of the Middle Ages.
  • Pigments came from mineral sources (lapis lazuli, cinnabar, orpiment) and organic dyes; gold and silver leaf adorned luxury manuscripts.
  • Modern analytical techniques (XRF, Raman spectroscopy, multispectral imaging) can identify pigments and inks non-invasively.

Medieval black ink falls into three main groups: carbon, iron-gall, and vegetable based or mixed inks. Carbon inks consisted of (1) soot or charcoal, (2) water, and (3) a binder such as tree gum, honey, gelatin, egg white, rabbit-skin glue, fats. Some carbon inks also have copper or lead ingredients. Iron-gall inkThe dominant writing ink of the medieval period, made from tannic acid (extracted from oak galls) combined with iron sulfate (green vitriol) and a binder such as gum arabic. consisted of (1) tannic acid extracted by boiling or macerating and filtering crushed gall nuts soaked in water, vinegar, wine, or beer, (2) a mineral source, green vitriol (iron sulfate) or metallic iron dissolved in acid, and (3) a binding like gum arabic. The mixture of tannic acid and iron leads to a chemical reaction, which gives the resultant ink corrosive properties. The category of vegetable or mixed inks is perhaps the least studied group and contains a variety of ink-types: (a) vegetable extract combined with a metallic salt; (b)vegetable extract with a binder; or (c) metallic salt with a binder. These vegetable or mixed inks might include tannic extracts, but did not undergo any chemical reaction because iron was absent from the mixture. Carbon inks were used in classical antiquity and again after the twelfth century. Iron-gall ink was the main ink type of the whole Middle Ages, but plant-based or mixed inks were also used.

After the main text was transcribed, the manuscript could be rubricated. Rubrics refer to texts, usually written in red, that mark titles, headings, section breaks, or sense divisions. They have a function essential to the main text. The term comes from the Latin rubricare, "to colour red," and reflects how scribes used red ink for textual elements that guided readers through the text. Rubricators often worked separately from the main scribe, and, in many surviving manuscripts, rubrication spaces are left blank where work was never completed. Some manuscripts also use rubrics for an aesthetic effect.

Beyond red ink, decorative practice expanded into the use of coloured initials. Small flourishing capitals in penwork were common in Gothic books, often executed in alternating red and blue and filled with intricate fleurons, interlace, or figural motifs. In more luxurious works these initials could be large, multicoloured, or even historiatedHistoriated initials contain figurative scenes (human or animal figures) that relate to the adjacent text, as opposed to purely decorative initials. — containing figurative scenes that related to the text.

Apart from initials, codices could be decorated with miniatures, illustrations, borders and other embellishments. Until about 1200, illustrations or miniatures were often executed within the monastic house creating the codex. From the late Middle Ages, major illustration campaigns were executed by professional workshops or ateliers. However, not all decoration was pictorial. Many manuscripts feature calligraphic ornament, marginal embellishment, and decorative frames that enhance readability as much as aesthetic appeal. These features often embodied theological and mnemonic functions: borders, symbolic forms, or animal figures could frame text in ways that reinforced memory or devotional experience. In this late twelfth-century manuscript from the monastery of Weissenau the illustrator depicts himself painting the initial, giving glimpse to a workstation outfitted with an assortment of inkhorns for different colors.

The illustrator illustrates himself at his workstation, painting an initial. Cologny, Fondation Martin Bodmer, Cod. Bodmer 127 (Passionary of Weissenau), fol. 244r. Image available via e-Codices.

Pigments for colored initials and for illustrations were drawn from various mineral and organic sources: red from cinnabar and vermilion, blue from azurite, ultramarine blue from lapis lazuli, dark blue from indigo, white from gypsum or calcium compounds, black from chars and soot, yellow from orpiment, greens from malachite or copper sulphate, and metal leaf made of thinly hammered gold and silver sheets. These pigments were bound with organic media such as egg tempera or gum arabic. In 2019, a team of researchers announced the discovery of blue pigment on the tooth of a nun who lived in the women's monastery at Dalheim, Germany, between 997 C.E. and 1162 C.E. Their analysis revealed that blue pigment was caused by ground lapis lazuli, a precious mineral sourced only in a certain region of Afghanistan. This unexpected finding reflects the long trade routes standing behind book production. Perhaps more interesting, it reveals that this nun was closely involved in a practice that came into contact with ground lapis lazuli. In all likelihood, she was a scribe and illustrator.

Scholars and conservators today can analyze the material composition of inks and pigments using a range of non- or minimally invasive technologies that reveal the chemical and mineral content without damaging fragile codices. Infrared reflectography can distinguished carbon and iron-gall inks from one another. Techniques such as X-ray fluorescence (XRF)A non-destructive analytical technique that bombards a surface with X-rays to identify elemental composition. Widely used in manuscript studies to detect metallic pigments (lead white, gold, vermilion) and other materials. allow researchers to detect the elemental make-up of metallic inks, pigments, and gold or silver leaf. Raman spectroscopy can identify molecular structures in organic and inorganic colourants, and distinguishing plant-based dyes, earth pigments, and synthetic compounds. Infrared and multispectral imaging highlight layers of pigments invisible to the naked eye, revealing erased, faded, or underdrawn decorations and clarifying the sequence of application.

Unit VI Sewing the Bookblock

📜 Key Takeaways

  • Holes were made in the pacrhment to set sewing stations where sewing supports (leather thongs or hemp/linen cords) were sewn to the book block. These supports create the bond between the book block and the book cover.
  • From at least the High Middle Ages, sewing took place on a sewing frame that held the supports under tension.
  • Integral sewing combined quire sewing and endband sewing in a single pass/

The copied book was now ready to be bound. First, the book block was prepared for sewing. Each quire — composed of folded sheets of parchment or paper nested together — was opened at its center fold. Perhaps using an awl or penknife, the binder pierced a series of holes or cut small slits along the fold. These sewing stations were carefully measured to align across all gatherings. Only in the late Middle Ages do the number of sewing stations seem to relate to the size and weight of the codex.

Once pierced, the quires were refolded and arranged in correct order to form the bookblock. The gatherings were then sewn together along thicker cords or thongs known as sewing supportsCords or thongs (of leather, alum-tawed skin, hemp, or linen) stretched across the spine onto which quires are sewn. They form the structural backbone of medieval bindings and are later laced into the bookboards.. These supports formed the structural backbone of the binding. In earlier periods they were often made of split leather or alum-tawed skin; later, cords of hemp or linen became common. The sewing thread, perhaps coated in wax to make sewing easier, passed in and out through the prepared holes, looping around the supports at each station. This technique linked the gatherings together while simultaneously anchoring them to the supports.

From at least the High Middle Ages onward, sewing commonly took place on a sewing frameA wooden device used by medieval binders to hold sewing supports (cords or thongs) taut under tension during the sewing of quires. The supports were stretched vertically between upper and lower beams; the binder passed the needle through each gathering in turn, looping the thread around each taut support.. If we look again at the Bamberg scribe illustration, we see what is perhaps the only illustration before 1400 of a sewing frame. Presumably, the illustrator expected people to recognize the sewing frame depicted there much as they should recognize the other illustrations on the same page. Nevertheless, it is unclear to modern viewers how it works in detail. In particular, while the sewing supports are attached by a mechanism, it is unclear what this mechanism is. In any case, the book block, the stack of copied quires, would sit on this frame during the next part of the process.

Illustration of the Creation of a Book (c. 1200). Bamberg, Staatsbibliothek, Msc.Patr.5. Digital image provided by the digital holdings of the Bamberg Staatsbibliothek.

This sewing frame held the sewing supports under tension, stretched vertically between the upper and lower beams of the frame. In the picture below, sewing supports are hanging from the cross beam of the sewing frame.

Modern reproduction of a sewing frame. Image from Conservation Wiki.

The binder passed the needle through each gathering in turn and around the taut supports. Many different stitching styles were employed: herringbone, straight, packed straight, and others.

Stitch styles on a slit leather strap: herringbone, straight, packed straight. Drawn by N. Brockey after Szirmai.

The thread ran on the inside of the center of a quire to the next station. When the gatherings were sewn together, the thread looped around these supports, forming raised bands along the spine. At the end of the quire, the thread exited and moved to the next quire by a link ("kettle") or span stitch.

A trivial number of Romanesque bindings and a more significant, albeit not large, number of Gothic bindings exhibit integral sewing — a technique undoubtedly invented to accelerate the tedious work of endband sewing. Instead of stopping short of the spine end to make the changeover, the binder runs the sewing thread all the way to the head or tail of the spine and loops it around the endband support cable.

Unit VII Spine Treatment, Endbands & Edge Trimming

📜 Key Takeaways

  • Endbands (sewn at head and tail of the spine) stabilized gatherings and protected spine edges from wear; in many medieval bindings they were also laced into the bookboards.
  • Carolingian and Romanesque spines tend to be flat or concave; Gothic spines tend to be convex, possibly from deliberate rounding with a hammer.
  • Spine linings of parchment, linen, or leather reinforced the spine and provided a base for covering materials.
  • Edge trimming — with a drawknife in earlier periods, and a book plough from the later Middle Ages onward — gave the bookblock smooth, even edges.

The handling of the spine changes during the Middle Ages. While the profile of Carolingian and Romanesque bindings tends to show a flat or concave spine, Gothic bindings tend to have a round, convex spine. Following other scholars, Szirmai (pp. 193–194) infers that Gothic bindings owe their convex shape to deliberate rounding of the spine, possibly carried out with a hammer. For, after sewing, the spine of the book block often exhibits "swelling" caused by the accumulation of layered thread and the thickness of folded parchment gatherings. According to Szirmai's interpretation, controlled hammer blows helped reshape the spine to reduce this swelling. In some cases faint traces of this shaping process can still be observed in the profile of surviving books.

In all periods, the top and bottom of the spine were reinforced by the addition of endbandsStructural and decorative elements at the head (top) and tail (bottom) of a book spine. In their earliest form they were sewn through the gatherings onto a core of thong or cord, stabilizing the gatherings and protecting the spine edges.. In their earliest and most structural form, they were sewn directly through the gatherings at the head (top) and tail (bottom) of the spine. A core of leather thong, rolled parchment, or cord was laid along the edge of the spine, forming a single or double cable, and thread was worked around it in a series of tight stitches that passed through the folded sections of the textblock. This sewing helped stabilize the gatherings, preventing them from shifting and protecting the vulnerable spine edges from wear as the book was handled. In many medieval bindings, the endbands were also laced and pegged into the top spine corners of the book boards. Over time, endbands became more elaborate, with colored threads forming decorative patterns, though their structural role remained essential as in earlier periods.

Modern enband sewing with two colors of thread. Available via Conservation Wiki.

Once the endbands were completed, attention turned to the spine lining. This was an added layer of parchment, linen or leather support pasted to the spine with flour paste or, in the late Middle Ages, animal glue. The support was often cut with spaces for the raised sewing supports. The leftover support on either side could likewise be pasted onto the board: inside, outside, or, by cutting multiple flaps, both inside and outside of the board.

Different ways of pasting spine linings to boards: no attachment, on the outside of the board, on the inside of the board, on both the inside and outside. The illustration shows the rounded, convex spine of a Gothic book. Drawn by N. Brockey after Szirmai.

In the picture below, a parchment folio has been repurposed as a spine lining. The cutting pattern has earned it the name of a "comb" lining. The two fragments met on the book spine; the "windows" cut out allowed for the sewing supports.

A twelfth-century parchment folio used as spine lining. Budapest, Magyar Tudományos Akadémia Könyvtár és Információs Központ, T 338. Conservation Wiki.

This additional layer served multiple purposes: it reinforced the spine, distributed mechanical stress during opening, and provided a stable base for further covering materials. In heavier volumes, additional linings of parchment or leather might be added to increase strength.

Edge trimming was another important stage in the finishing of the bookblock. As mentioned earlier, trimming was probably done during the early Middle Ages after the boards were attached to the book block. In the late Middle Ages, trimming took place before the boards were attached, ensuring that the pages were even and square. The binder secured the bookblock firmly and used a sharp drawknife to shave the edges smooth by hand. This procedure required care, as uneven pressure could gouge or tear the parchment. In the late medieval and early modern periods, the book plough appeared for this task. This illustration from 1568 shows the bookbinder trimming the book with a book plough.

An illustration of a bookbinder trimming the edge of the book block. Jost Amman and Hans Sachs, Eygentliche Beschreibung aller Stände auff Erden, hoher und nidriger, geistlicher und weltlicher, aller Künsten, Handwercken und Händeln ... (Frankfurt, 1568). Wikimedia Commons.

This device was a vise-like press that held the book block in place as a blade was repeatedly drawn across it. At the feet of the bookbinder lies a drawknife. Smoothly trimmed edges not only improved the book's appearance but also reduced the likelihood of dirt accumulation.

Unit VIII Bookboards

📜 Key Takeaways

  • Bookboards were typically made of oak, though beech, pine, walnut, poplar, and others were also used.
  • Sewing supports were laced through channels drilled into the boards.
  • In the Carolingian period, boards were often attached when the book block was sewn together; from the Gothic period, they were attached after sewing and trimming.
  • Board edges could be squared, rounded, or beveled, depending on purpose and local practice.

The covers of medieval codices were typically made from oak boards. Other kinds of wood were available: beech, pine, walnut, poplar. A minor, but significant, body of medieval books has covers bound in parchment, often called "limp bindings," sometimes layered into thick covers. The thickness of wooden book boards varied across periods and could relate to the size of the book and the eminence of the design. A private prayer book might have a thinner cover than a luxurious liturgical book. The edges could be squared, rounded, or beveled in varying degrees along the spine edge, the free edges, or both. Board shaping could also be applied to only part of an edge. In the late Middle Ages, the spine edge was usually beveled or rounded to facilitate the lacing of the sewing supports and to accommodate the extra width of the book block at the spine edge, where the support is folded and thread run through.

Wooden board binding showing laced-in sewing supports. Drawn by N. Brockey.

Channels were drilled into the boards to match the sewing supports along the spine and the endbands at head and tail. Using awls, drills, gimlets, or knives, the binder created angled tunnels or grooves in the board. Different patterns were employed. To attach the boards, the binder threaded or "laced" the loose ends of the sewing supports through the channels. The channels were often angled so that the supports entered from the spine edge and emerged on the inside of the board. The ends were then frayed, wedged, or secured in place with small wooden pegs or adhesive. This lacing techniqueThe method by which sewing support ends were threaded through channels drilled in the wooden boards and then frayed, wedged, or pegged in place. Laced-board bindings were structurally far stronger than the adhesive case-bindings that largely replaced them after the early modern period. created a strong bond between book block and board. Unlike later case bindings, in which covers were attached with adhesive alone, medieval laced-board bindings relied on this structural integration for durability.

There was no single method of medieval bookbinding, and book boards illustrate that aptly. In the early medieval period, particularly during the Carolingian eraThe Carolingian period (c. 750–900 C.E.) refers to the dynasty of Charlemagne (r. 768–814 C.E.) and his successors. It was marked by a major cultural and educational revival (the "Carolingian Renaissance") with important consequences for book production and script standardization. (8th–9th centuries), binders often prepared the boards in advance and attached them when the quires of the book block were sewn. The sewing supports were laced into one cover, the quires were sewn, and then the sewing supports were laced into the other cover. The book block was then trimmed with the boards on it, as revealed by scraping marks left by trimming on the edges of some boards. This trimming created a smooth, even edge. From perhaps the twelfth century on, the use of the sewing frame allowed binders to attach the boards at a later stage. In the Romanesque period, book trimming still occurred after the boards had been attached. In the Gothic period, the book block was trimmed before it was attached to the boards.

Unit IX Covering, Fastenings & Embellishment

📜 Key Takeaways

  • Boards were covered in leather (calf, sheep, goat, pig, deer, or seal), alum-tawed skin, textiles, or, for limp bindings, parchment.
  • Clasps and straps were a functional necessity: parchment expands and contracts with humidity and had to be kept tightly closed to prevent warping.
  • Cover decoration ranged from blind tooling on tanned leather to precious stones, gold or silver filigree, and enamel on luxury books.
  • Bosses and corner pieces protected boards from abrasion; chemise bindings and overcovers provided additional protection for traveling books.

The book boards were covered in leather — calf, sheep, goat, deer, pig, even seal — prepared in a variety of ways: alum-tawingA method of preparing animal skin by treating it with alum and salt, producing a white, firm, flexible leather that does not rot in the same way as vegetable-tanned leather. Widely used for sewing supports and some book covers in the Middle Ages., oil or fat tanning, tanning. Sometimes textiles were used in place of a leather product. The covering material was tightly applied and pasted down with vegetable paste or, more commonly in the later Middle Ages, with animal glue. The cover was folded over the inside book board, cut at the corners, and pasted down. They could be mitredMitring (or mitering): cutting the leather at the corners of the book board at 45° angles so that the folded material meets neatly without bulking or overlapping. Standard practice for a tidy, professional cover finish., so that the folded material at the right angles was cut to form two 45° angles, and they could be sewn together along this angle.

Covers were decorated with a seemingly endless array of techniques and motifs. Blind toolingThe impression of heated metal tools or stamps into leather to create decorative designs, without the addition of gold or colour. Common on tanned leather book covers from the Romanesque period onward. — the impression of a pattern or image through heated metal stamps and tools — might be applied to tanned leathers. These designs could include geometric interlace, foliage, rosettes, or small animal motifs. Luxury books, such as Gospel and liturgical books, could be decorated with precious stones and metals. Stones such as garnets, sapphires, amethysts, rock crystal and pearls, had to be polished as cabochonA gemstone shaped by polishing into a smooth, convex, unfaceted form. Such work required skilled craftspeople and depended on extensive supply chains.

Fastenings were another essential feature of a finished binding. Because medieval manuscripts were written on parchment, a hygroscopic material that expands and contracts with humidity, codices needed to be held tightly closed to prevent warping. In the Carolingian period, a short leather strap with a metal eye, fixed on one cover, was fastened over a metal peg or stud on the opposite cover. The mechanism in the Romanesque period was similar, except that the predominant method used a longer strap, with a fixture further recessed into the board. This method persisted in the Gothic period. At the same time, a new method appeared: a metal clasp fixed to one board snapped over the lip of a catch plate attached to the other board, as illustrated here.

Gothic clasps on a German book from 1479. Image available via Conservation Wiki.

One end of the strap was anchored to one cover, while the metal end of the clasp hooked onto a catch plate fixed to the other cover. Clasps could be simple brass hooks or more ornate pieces engraved with decorative patterns. On large choir books, the fastenings were substantial, sometimes featuring multiple clasps along the fore edge.

Bosses and corner pieces, though primarily protective, also contributed to the cover's appearance. Raised metal bossesConvex metal fittings (often brass or bronze) fixed to the front and back covers of a book. When the book was laid flat on a table, these bosses prevented abrasion of the leather on the table surface. Corner pieces shielded edges from wear and tear. Often made of brass or bronze, they could be plain or embellished, sometimes to match the other metal book furnishings.

Overcovers provided an additional layer of protection, particularly for books that traveled. These could be made of cloth or secondary leather wrappings. Common in monastic and later medieval contexts., where an extended leather flap folded over the fore edge and sometimes tied shut. In other cases, embroidered fabric covers were slipped over precious volumes.
